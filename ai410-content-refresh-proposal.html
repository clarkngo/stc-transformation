AI 410 Content Refresh: Teaching Notes from the Reading

Eight small additions to the AI 410 course pages, drawn from Clark's picks in the sociomateriality reading guide. The syllabus, rubrics, grade weights, and deliverables stay exactly as they are.

📅 Nov 16, 2026 ✏️ 8 changes, all wording or guidance 🔒 Syllabus and rubrics unchanged 🗂️ Current version kept for reference
BSAI reminder: the standing rules for AI 410 / 420 / 430 / 440 are in the course design constraints. Nothing here changes the official description or outcomes, Codespaces, free tiers, or the two-week HOS schedule.

In short

  • AI 410 already teaches the core idea. Every HOS runs Create/Scaffold → Evaluate ("the critical gate") → Analyze → Understand & Refine ("by hand, no AI"). The reading mostly confirms the design.
  • What's missing is the why. Students see the rules but not the reasons. These changes make the reasons visible, where students are working.
  • Nothing new is graded or submitted. Every change is page wording, an ungraded step, or guidance on something students already hand in.

Example wordings

What students see now, and what the new version would say

Module 1 is the sample throughout; Modules 2–4 follow the same pattern with their own details. The new wording is drafted on the v1.1-content-refresh branch of the ai410 repo, so anything here can be cut or rewritten before it's merged.

1 · Name what students think AI is · course home, above the module list
Now

Nothing. The page goes straight from the course intro to the list of modules.

New wording

Before Module 1: three questions for yourself
Two minutes, nothing to submit. Answer in your head or in a note, and look again at the end of the term.

  1. In one sentence, what is AI to you right now: something that gives answers, something you work with, or something else?
  2. Why might this course let you use AI in some stages and not in others?
  3. When an AI writes part of your code, what do you still need to be able to do yourself?

Many people start out treating AI as an answer machine. In this course it's a fast first draft that you test, explain and extend. Every HOS is built that way: it starts with AI and ends by hand.

2 · Label AI's role in every activity · each module page, in each activity's header
Now

HOS 1 · Stage 2 of 4

Headers show only the stage. Understand & Refine is the one activity whose header says anything about AI ("No AI assistant for this part"); for the rest, the AI Use rules are stated elsewhere, not next to the activity.

New wording

HOS 1 · Stage 2 of 4AI: your judgment

The full set of tags:

AI: expected Create / Scaffold
AI: your judgment Evaluate
AI: your own words Analyze
No AI Understand & Refine, Knowledge check
AI: think, don't write Muddiest Point, Concept Debate
AI: allowed, disclose Application Challenge

With one line at the top of the lesson: "Each activity below is tagged with its AI rule, from AI: expected in Create / Scaffold to No AI in Understand & Refine and the Knowledge Check."

3 · Plan before you prompt · HOS guide and lesson, top of Create / Scaffold
Now

"Get a working app running fast with an AI coding assistant. This is the starting point, not the deliverable — you don't hand-edit anything yet."

New wording (added above it)

Plan before you prompt · 2 minutes, ungraded. Before you paste the prompt, jot a few lines of your own plan: which files you expect, where the key logic should live, and one thing you think the assistant might get wrong. When it's done, compare. Where its version differs from yours is where to look first in Evaluate. Nothing to submit; this doesn't change how Create / Scaffold is graded.

4 · Show how professionals verify · HOS guide and lesson, Evaluate stage
Now

"The critical gate — is the AI-produced result actually correct?"

New wording (added below it)

Why it counts: checking AI output is what working developers do. In Stack Overflow's developer surveys, the share of professional developers using AI tools rose to 85% by 2026, but only about 4 in 10 of them trusted its accuracy, and 98% said they check its output before using it (Makara & Chhim, 2026).

5 · Explain why the by-hand stage exists · HOS guide and lesson, Understand & Refine
Now

"By hand this time — no AI assistance for this stage."

New wording (added below it)

Why by hand? You can only judge AI output against know-how of your own, and that know-how comes from doing the work yourself. This stage is where it forms.

6 · Treat snags as learning moments · HOS guide, Common snags; lesson, Evaluate
Now

A list of snags with fixes, no introduction. For example:

"The agent always calls the tool, even when it shouldn't — check the tool's description in its schema. A vague description ("does math") makes the model over-eager; a specific one narrows it down."

New wording

A new opening line: "These all came up in testing, so expect to hit one or two. A snag doesn't mean you did it wrong. Most show how the system really works, and the ones marked What it teaches make good material for your Analyze or Understand writing."

The same snag, with one line added: "What it teaches: the model routes on the tool's description. The description is part of your code."

In Evaluate: "A test that breaks the app is a good result here: it shows you exactly where the AI's code is weakest."

7 · Fresh discussion prompts · Concept Debate example page, new optional section
Now

Only each module's graded question, e.g. "When a request might need a tool, who should decide — the model, or deterministic backend code?" That stays as it is.

New wording (optional, ungraded)

Your agent emails a customer the wrong refund amount. Who is responsible?
Side A: the people who built and deployed it. An agent is software, and its owners answer for what it does.
Side B: responsibility is shared. When an agent decides for itself, the model provider and the company that set its limits share the blame.

When every student can use AI, do grades still show skill?
Side A: yes, if the work checks judgment rather than output: testing, explaining, and changing code by hand.
Side B: less and less. If AI can produce the work, a grade says little about the person, and employers will look for other proof.

8 · Trace which tool did what · HOS guide and lesson, under the Evaluate note
Now

The note's prompt: "What I tested · what I found · what the AI got wrong, missed, or handled surprisingly well"

New wording (added below it)

A line worth adding to your Evaluate note: which tests you came up with, which (if any) an AI suggested, and which problems you found yourself. It makes your own judgment easy to see.

A student's line might read: "I wrote the divide-by-zero and empty-input tests; Copilot suggested the large-number test. I found the missing error message myself."

What stays fixed

The line every change has to stay behind
  • The syllabus (the OneDrive master), the rubrics, grade weights, and due dates.
  • What students submit and where: HOS work is still pushed to the Classroom50 repo, with no new Brightspace items.
  • The AI Use rules per activity. The changes explain the rules; they don't loosen or tighten them.
  • Knowledge-check banks and the Term Project phases.
Test for each change: if a student skipped it entirely, would their grade change? If yes, it's out of scope.

Keeping the current version

So the old version stays available for reference

Recommended: before any edit, tag the current state of the cityuseattle/ai410 repo as v1.0 and publish a GitHub release with a zip of the site. Then make the changes as v1.1 in one pull request.

  • Why a tag, not a second copy of the site: a copy published next to the live course would give students two versions to find in search. A tag keeps v1.0 exact and easy to compare (git diff v1.0) without confusing anyone.
  • If you also want to browse it: the release zip opens locally in any browser.
  • Rollback: any single change can be reverted on its own, or the whole site restored to v1.0.
