The Term Project Interview: Run It Like a Job Interview

A 15-minute one-on-one in Week 9 or 10, worth half of the Term Project (200 of its 400 points, 20% of the course grade), that feels like a technical interview for an AI engineering role. The interviewer leads with questions and the student answers. There are no slides and no prepared walkthrough, and the student shows the system only when asked. This page has the format, the rubric, the instructor's preparation and question bank, and the student prep guide. To run the interviews, use the instructor guide.

In short

  • Interviewer-led, not presenter-led. It opens with "Tell me about your project in 60 seconds" and moves straight to questions. Screen sharing happens only when the interviewer says "show me".
  • Six stages, like a real interview loop: the pitch, a deep dive on one request path, design trade-offs, a debugging scenario, ownership and AI use, then the student's own questions.
  • Five criteria in the same four-level grid as Scott's rubrics: Technical Understanding 25%, Design Reasoning 25%, Debugging 20%, Ownership & AI Transparency 15%, Professional Communication 15%. Each level also reads as a hiring signal, from Strong hire to No hire.
  • Weight (decided Nov 24; placed inside the Term Project Nov 25): 200 points, one 15-minute interview in Week 9 or 10. The Term Project is 40%: its team deliverables keep their v1 points (200) and the individual Interview is the other 200. It was briefly listed as a separate “Faculty Interview” category (Nov 24); since Nov 25 it is part of the Term Project, under the name Interview. The total stays 1,000, and AIC, CD and KC don't change. Details below.
  • Every interview is tailored from the student's own repo, commits, Post Mortems and Progress Report, using a one-page interview card. It takes about 10 minutes of prep per student.
  • Students prepare like a job candidate: a 60-second pitch, one request path they can trace end to end, three decisions with the alternatives they rejected, one "it broke and here's how I fixed it" story, and a question to ask back.

Format: a 15-minute technical interview

Book 20-minute slots: 15 to talk, 5 to score while it's fresh
0–2 min1. The pitch"Tell me about your project like you would to a hiring manager." No screen.
2–7 min2. Deep diveThe interviewer picks one request path; the student traces it. "Show me" only here.
7–10 min3. Trade-offs"Why X and not Y? What did you give up?"
10–12 min4. DebuggingA short "it's broken in production" scenario.
12–14 min5. Ownership & AIWhat's yours, what the AI wrote, what you changed by hand.
14–15 min6. Your questions"What would you ask me?" or "What would you build next?"

Ground rules

What makes it an interview

  • The interviewer asks and the student answers. Answers run 30–90 seconds, not 5 minutes.
  • The student has the running system, the repo in Codespaces and one Langfuse trace open but not shared until asked.
  • Follow-ups go deeper: "Why?", "What else did you consider?", "What would break?"
  • "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" is a good answer.
  • Live, on camera or in person. Same six stages for every student.

What makes it a presentation (avoid)

  • Slides, a scripted walkthrough, or a demo the student drives from start to finish.
  • Reading from notes or the README.
  • The interviewer filling silences or rescuing a stuck answer straight away. Give it 10 seconds.
  • Questions any classmate could answer from the course pages. Ask about this system.
  • Any AI assistant open during the interview (the AI Use Guide already says "No AI").

Groups: each member is interviewed alone. At least one deep-dive question is about a part a teammate built. In a real team you're expected to know the seams. Use the same scenario for every member of a team so their answers can be compared.

Technical Understanding (25%) Traces the chosen request end to end without notes, through the client, API, agent loop, tools, retrieval, worker and trace. Names the actual files and functions, and explains why each step happens in that order. Traces the request correctly with minor gaps, and points to the right code when asked to show it. Describes the components but not how a request moves between them, or needs the README to find the code. Can't explain how the system works, or the explanation contradicts the code. Missing interview earns 0 points.
Design Reasoning (25%) Justifies key decisions against stated criteria such as cost, latency, reliability or safety, names the rejected alternative, and predicts what would break first under change (10× users, a new document type, a model outage). Justifies decisions with a reason and an alternative considered; the prediction under change is reasonable but general. Gives reasons such as "it was in the guide" or "the AI suggested it", with no alternatives. Can't say why the system is built the way it is.
Debugging & Problem Solving (20%) Given a failure scenario, forms a hypothesis, names the first place to look (log, trace, queue, test), and narrows it systematically. Reads a live trace or log confidently when asked. Finds a sensible starting point and reasons toward the cause, with some prompting. Guesses, or jumps to rewriting code without first locating the fault. Has no approach to the failure, even with hints.
Ownership & AI Transparency (15%) States clearly which parts they built, which parts the AI generated and what they changed by hand, matching the commits and the AI Tool Disclosure. Knows a teammate's seam well enough to work on it. Connects a Post Mortem "carry forward" item to the project. Ownership and AI use are clear and consistent with the repo; knowledge of teammates' parts is partial. Vague about what they built or what the AI wrote, or answers don't fully match the commit history. Can't explain code they're credited with, or claims don't match the repo. The instructor follows up under the Academic Integrity policy, not in the room.
Professional Communication (15%) A crisp 60-second pitch (problem, user, what it does, what makes it AI). Answers are concise and structured, the student asks clarifying questions, handles "I don't know" honestly and asks a thoughtful question back. Clear pitch and answers, with occasional rambling; asks a question back. Answers are hard to follow, or the pitch turns into a feature list or a scripted presentation. Mostly unable to communicate the work, or reads from a script.

Score each criterion at its level's midpoint (96 / 83 / 68 / 30%) unless the notes clearly put it higher or lower in the band. The hiring-signal labels help students understand the level; the grade comes from the percentage.

How instructors prepare

About 1 hour once per term, then about 10 minutes per student
Running the interviews? The instructor guide has the term timeline, what to say in each stage, scoring with a worked example, sample answers at each level, keys for the debugging scenarios and what to do in awkward situations.

Once per term (start of Week 9)

  • Post the interview sign-up slots in Brightspace for Weeks 9–10 (20-minute slots), with the student prep guide below and the rubric attached. Module 5 already tells students to watch for them.
  • Pick the scenario set for the term (the debugging scenarios below) so every student gets one of the same few. Rotate the sets between terms so they don't spread.
  • If two instructors share a section, both score the first three interviews together and compare, then split the rest.
  • Check accommodations with Disability Support Services before the first slot.

For each student (about 10 minutes, the day before)

  1. Read the README and architecture diagram. Choose one request path to trace, ideally one that crosses two AICs (for example, a question that triggers retrieval and a tool call, or an upload that goes through the worker and ends in a push notification).
  2. Skim the commits by this student. In a group, pick one file they own and one seam with a teammate's part.
  3. Read their four Post Mortems. Note one "still unclear" item and one "carry forward" item to ask about.
  4. Read the Progress Report and AI Tool Disclosure. Note one stated decision to probe, and one AI-generated part to ask about.
  5. Fill in the interview card (below) with three tailored questions and the scenario. Don't send questions in advance.

Question bank

Pick one or two per stage and tailor them to the student's system. Don't publish this list; students get sample questions only.

1. The pitch

  • Tell me about your project in 60 seconds, as if I were a hiring manager who has never seen it.
  • Who is the user, and what were they doing before your system existed?
  • What makes this an AI system, rather than a regular app with a chatbot added?

2. Deep dive (by AIC)

AreaQuestions
AIC 1 · Agent loop & toolsShow me where the model decides to call a tool. What does the model actually receive back, and what happens if the tool throws? How many turns can the loop take, and what stops it? Why does the API key live in the backend and not the client?
AIC 2 · RAGWalk me from an uploaded document to a stored vector. How did you choose chunk size and overlap? Why are documents and queries embedded differently? Show me an answer and the chunks it was grounded in. What does the system do when nothing relevant is retrieved?
AIC 3 · Guardrails & asyncShow me your validation schema. What happens to a malformed tool call, step by step? Why is ingestion a background job? If the worker is down, what does the user see? Where could the same job run twice, and does that matter?
AIC 4 · Mobile, push & observabilityHow does the phone find out the job is done? Show me a trace: which step was slowest, and which cost the most tokens? How does your groundedness score work, and when would it be wrong?
Seams (groups)Your teammate built the retrieval. What does your code assume about what it returns? If they changed the chunking tomorrow, what in your part would break?

3. Design trade-offs

  • Name one decision you'd defend in a design review. What did you reject, and why?
  • You're going from 10 users to 10,000 tomorrow. What breaks first, and what do you change?
  • What may the agent do on its own, and where does a person have to approve? How did you draw that line (your AIC challenge decisions)?
  • Where did you choose free-tier tools? What would you change with a budget, and what wouldn't you?
  • If the model provider went down for an hour, what would your users see?

4. Debugging scenarios (pick one, the same within a team)

  1. Users say answers have become confidently wrong since yesterday's document upload. Where do you look first?
  2. The push notification never arrives, but the job shows as finished. Talk me through it.
  3. Latency doubled overnight and nobody changed the code. What do you check?
  4. The model keeps calling a tool with an invalid date and the guardrail retries forever. What's happening, and how do you stop it?
  5. Open a trace from your system now and tell me something you didn't expect to see. (Use when the student is strong.)

5. Ownership & AI use

  • Which part of this code would you be most comfortable changing right now, live? (Optionally ask for a one-line change.)
  • What did an AI assistant write here, and what did you have to fix or rewrite by hand? How did you know it was wrong?
  • Your Post Mortem said [X] was still unclear after AIC [n]. Is it clear now? What changed?
  • Tell me about a time in this project when something broke badly. What happened, what did you do, and what was the result? (Situation, Task, Action, Result.)

6. Their questions

  • What question would you ask me about this system if you were interviewing for my team?
  • What would you build next with two more weeks, and why that first?
🎙️

During and after the interview

Keep it a conversation; score straight after

During

  • Open warmly and state the format: "This is like a technical interview. I'll ask, you answer, and I'll tell you when to share your screen. It's fine to think out loud or ask me to clarify."
  • Use the follow-up ladder on one or two answers: Why? → What else did you consider? → What would break? Stop when the student reaches the edge of what they know. That edge is the information you need.
  • Watch the clock and move on at each stage's time, even mid-answer: "Good, let's switch to…"
  • Write evidence, not impressions: "traced upload → worker → Chroma, missed where embeddings are batched", not "seemed solid".
  • Don't teach mid-interview. Save corrections for the feedback note. If a student is stuck for more than 20 seconds, give one hint, then move on.

After (the 5 minutes in the slot)

  • Score each criterion from the notes on the card, and pick the hiring signal.
  • Write the feedback like an interview debrief: one strength, one thing to work on, two sentences each. Post it in Brightspace within two days.
  • If claims and the repo don't match, don't decide in the room. Check the commits and the AI Tool Disclosure afterwards, and use the Academic Integrity process if needed.
  • Enter the score in the interview's own grade item. Also use the notes as evidence when you check ownership against the commits for TP03.

Student guide: preparing for your Term Project interview

Written to go on the Module 5 page as it is

What it is: a 15-minute, one-on-one technical interview about your Term Project, in Week 9 or 10. It's worth 200 points (20% of your grade) and is scored on your own answers, even if you work in a group. Treat it like an interview for an AI engineering job. Your instructor asks the questions; you answer them, and you show your system when asked. It is not a presentation: no slides, and no prepared walkthrough.

Before: what to prepare

  1. A 60-second pitch. The problem, who the user is, what your system does, and what makes it an AI system. Practise it out loud until it fits in a minute.
  2. One request, end to end. Pick a request that crosses at least two AICs and trace it on paper: client → API → agent loop → tool or retrieval → worker → notification → trace. Be ready to open each file on that path.
  3. Three decisions you'd defend. For each one: what you chose, what you rejected, and why. Your AIC challenge decisions and Progress Report are good sources.
  4. One "it broke" story. Something that really went wrong, told as Situation, Task, Action, Result, in under a minute.
  5. Your AI use, honestly. Which parts an assistant wrote, what you changed by hand, and how you checked it. It should match your commits and your README's AI Tool Disclosure.
  6. Your Post Mortems. Re-read all four. Expect a question about something you said was unclear, and where your "carry forward" items went.
  7. Your limits. What doesn't work yet, what would break at scale, and what you'd build next.
  8. A question to ask back, as you would at the end of any job interview.

Practise

  • Run a mock interview with a classmate, taking turns as interviewer. Use the sample questions below and ask "why?" after every answer.
  • Record one practice answer and listen back: aim for 30–90 seconds per answer.
  • In a group, interview each other about the parts you didn't build.

Sample questions (the real ones are tailored to your project)

  • Tell me about your project in 60 seconds.
  • Show me where your agent decides to call a tool.
  • Why did you choose that chunk size?
  • The push notification never arrives. Where do you look first?
  • What did the AI write here, and what did you change?

On the day

Have ready (10 minutes before)

  • Your Codespace running: API, worker and vector store up.
  • The app on your phone or emulator, logged in.
  • One recent Langfuse trace open.
  • The repo open in the editor. Don't share your screen until asked.
  • Camera on, quiet room, a working microphone.

Leave out

  • Slides or a scripted walkthrough.
  • Reading answers from notes or the README. A few keywords on paper are fine.
  • Any AI assistant, chat window or copilot (the AI Use Guide says "No AI" for the interview).
  • Bluffing. "I don't know, but here's how I'd find out" scores better than a confident wrong answer.

During

  • Think out loud: the reasoning counts as much as the answer.
  • Ask a clarifying question if one isn't clear. Interviewers expect it.
  • Keep answers short and structured; your interviewer will ask for more if they want it.

After: you'll get written feedback within two days, with one strength and one thing to work on, like an interview debrief. It's practice for the real thing.

Groups: everyone is interviewed alone. Know the whole system, and especially where your part meets your teammates' parts. Expect at least one question about a part you didn't build.
🤝

Fairness and accessibility

So the interview measures understanding, not polish or confidence
  • Same structure for everyone: the same six stages and timing, and the same scenario set. Only the tailored questions differ, and they come from each student's own work.
  • Judge content, not accent or fluency. Professional Communication is about clarity and structure. Allow pauses and repeat questions on request. Many BSAI students are working in a second language.
  • Accommodations through Disability Support Services: extra time, a written-first option (questions shown on screen), or a camera-off format. Record the accommodation on the card.
  • Interview anxiety is real: publishing the format, the rubric and sample questions in advance takes most of the surprise out. The first question is always the pitch the student has practised.
  • Technical failures: if Codespaces or the app won't start, carry on with the questions and use the code on GitHub. Don't mark down for infrastructure, and reschedule only if no code can be shown at all.
  • Recording: don't record by default. Record only with the student's written consent, for instructor calibration, and delete it after the term.
