0:00–2:00

1. Why we're here

AI changed two things at once: how students do the work, and how we can build a course.

  • For students: an AI assistant can now write working code for most assignments. If we grade "does the code run?", we're grading the AI, not the student.
  • For us: the same tools can draft lessons, guides, starter code, rubrics and quiz banks in minutes. Building a course is no longer limited by how fast one person can write.
Why it mattersAI 410 was designed around both changes, so it's a working example of what an AI-era course looks like, and of a faster way to build one. It's a model other STC courses can borrow from, not a one-off.

AI 410 at a glance: 10 weeks, 5 credits; four two-week hands-on units (HOS) where students build one AI assistant, then a Term Project. 23 published student pages, 4 Application Challenges, 175 Knowledge Check questions, a 1,000-point Brightspace gradebook. Built from Aug 25 to Oct 25, 2026.

3. Reversing Bloom's: the CEAU cycle

Bloom's taxonomy puts Create at the top, as the hardest thing a learner does. With AI, Create is now the fast, cheap part. The skill is in what comes after.

Traditional Bloom's (Create is the summit)

Create
Evaluate
Analyze
Apply
Understand
Remember

AI 410's order (Create comes first)

1 · Create / Scaffold (20%)Use an AI assistant to get a working version fast.
2 · EvaluateIs the AI's result actually right? Test it on purpose.
3 · AnalyzeExplain how one piece works, in your own words.
4 · Understand & RefineChange or extend it by hand, no AI. The mastery check.

Evaluate, Analyze and Understand & Refine together are 80% of each HOS grade.

Why we did itIf an AI can produce working code, grading the code no longer tells us whether the student understands it. Judging the AI's output, explaining it, and improving it by hand all require understanding, and they're hard to fake. We moved the grade to where the learning is.

Why not ban AI instead? Our graduates will use these tools at work on day one. Banning them would teach students to work in a way no employer expects, and it can't be enforced reliably anyway.

4. The Interactive Lesson Lab

The student site at cityuseattle.github.io/ai410. Students reach it from Brightspace; everything they submit still goes into Brightspace.

🗺️ Preview the build

Each module opens with a 15-minute guided tour of a simulator, so students see the whole unit working before the lecture.

🧪 Browser simulators

Four interactive models of the systems students build: no account, no code. Students break things on purpose and see why.

📖 Lessons

Written lecture notes with captioned videos, worked examples, common mistakes and quick checks, plus week tabs and due lists.

🛠️ Hands-on guides

Step-by-step builds in GitHub Codespaces on free tiers. Students need nothing installed and no credit card.

🎬 Term Project example

A fictional finished project students can click through, with its demo played step by step.

♿ Accessible and open

All pages meet WCAG 2.2 AA, checked automatically. Openly licensed (CC BY 4.0) so other courses can reuse them.

Why we did itMost students in the BSAI are new to software. Simulators let them practice and fail safely before touching real code, and they come to the lecture already knowing what to look for. A web page (rather than LMS content) loads the same everywhere, works on a phone, and can be fixed the same day.

Live look (2 minutes): open Module 1 → Preview the build → start the Agentic Loop Simulator tour → step 2, "Take the tool away", to show an AI answer that looks right and is wrong.

6. What AI unlocked, and the new bottleneck

AI didn't remove the bottleneck. It moved it.

What AI help unlocked

  • One faculty member built a full course in about nine weeks: 23 student pages, four simulators, eight starter-code templates, four Application Challenges and 175 Knowledge Check questions.
  • Redesigns stopped being expensive. The course changed shape twice (eight weekly units to four merged units, then two weeks per unit). Each reshape took days, not a term.
  • Things we couldn't staff before. Interactive simulators and guided tours normally need a developer; WCAG accessibility audits normally need a specialist. Both were built in.
  • Checking at a scale people can't. Line-by-line audits of the syllabus against the site, every rubric and every Brightspace item, which caught real mismatches before launch.
  • The LMS build itself. The gradebook, rubrics and quizzes were set up through Brightspace's API, with the faculty member signed in.
  • Same-day fixes. A wording error found in the morning is live on the student site that afternoon.

New limits we ran into

  • AI usage limits. Long working sessions hit the assistant's token and usage limits. Work has to be split into chunks, and each new session has to be brought back up to speed. Written decision notes and standing rules make that faster, but it's real time.
  • AI fluency is now a must. The assistant drafts quickly and can be confidently wrong. Faculty have to know how to direct it, check its output against the source, and catch a plausible error. This isn't optional for one course: it's a skill every STC faculty member needs.

What this taught us

  • Decide early. Rules that arrived mid-build (for example, two weeks per unit) caused the most rework. Write the non-negotiables down before any content exists.
  • Start the LMS early. The Brightspace build was the slowest, most manual step. Request the shell at the start, not the end.
  • Test as a student. A fresh-account run found a retired AI model that would have stopped every student on day one.

7. Questions we expect

Short answers to the questions most likely to come up. Click to open.

Isn't letting students use AI just cheating with permission?

AI is allowed where it's a tool (getting a first working version) and not where the learning is checked (Understand & Refine, done by hand, and Knowledge Checks, with a no-AI pledge). Most of the grade is for judging and explaining work, which AI can't do for the student.

How do you know a student did the "by hand" part?

Not with AI detectors, which are unreliable. Instructors use cheap, fair checks: ask the student to explain one line of their code, look for the work as its own commits in their repository, or ask for a small live change in office hours.

Does this replace faculty or instructional designers?

No. It moves their time. Every structural and grading decision was a faculty decision, and every change was reviewed before it went live. What shrank was the time spent typing, formatting and copying between systems.

How long did it take, and could I do this for my course?

About nine weeks of work by one faculty member, from first draft to a finished Brightspace shell, including two major redesigns. The process (decide, AI drafts, review, publish, audit) works for any course. The playbook lists the steps in order.

Do I need to be a programmer to build a course this way?

No, but you do need AI fluency: knowing how to give the assistant clear rules, review what it produces, and spot when it's wrong. That's the skill to build first, and it applies to every STC course, not just technical ones.

What does it cost students?

Nothing beyond tuition. Every unit runs in GitHub Codespaces on free tiers (Gemini, Upstash, Expo, Langfuse), with no credit card. Anything that would need a paid key is optional.

Is AI-generated course content accurate?

Only because of the checks around it. Faculty reviews every change, each hands-on unit gets a fresh-account test, automated checks catch broken links and accessibility problems, and audits compare the syllabus, site and Brightspace line by line. Those audits found real errors, such as rubric totals that didn't match assignment points, which were fixed before launch.

Our students aren't all technical. Will this overwhelm them?

That's the reason for the design. Pairing eight skills into four two-week units means fewer, deeper units instead of a new tool every week; simulators let students see a system working before they build it; and the AI assistant handles the boilerplate that used to stall beginners.

What happens when a free service or AI model changes?

It will. The model name is set in one place in the starter code, and the maintainer guide includes a pre-term re-test of every unit with fresh accounts.

Who maintains it if the course lead leaves?

The course lives in CityU's GitHub organization, not a personal account. There's an instructor runbook and a maintainer guide, and the Brightspace development shell can be copied into any new section.

Does it work with Brightspace, or replace it?

It works with it. Brightspace still holds enrollment, submissions, discussions, quizzes, rubrics and grades. Lessons open from Brightspace links.

Can other courses reuse the materials?

Yes. The student site is CC BY 4.0 and the starter code is MIT. AI 420, 430 and 440 already follow AI 410's structure, and the MP, CD and KC redesigns don't depend on the subject.
