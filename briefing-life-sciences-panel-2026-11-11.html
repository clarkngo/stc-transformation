The bullets to send

Short enough to read in the hallway before the panel. Paste into the Teams chat.
Ready to send. The lesson in question 4 (the WordPress course) comes from Clark recalling Sam's experience, so Sam may want to confirm or add to it.
  1. Can we contribute?
    • Yes, on talent and applied AI rather than lab science. "AI-driven biology" needs people who can build the data pipelines, ML models, cloud setups and security that the science runs on. That is what STC trains.
    • Our fit is working adults and career changers. With venture funding down about 58%, the region has scientists and staff who need to retool, and companies that need AI skills without adding headcount.
    • A second, less crowded opening is secure and compliant data handling. The China and biosecurity rules now shaping deal terms make data governance and cybersecurity a real need for early-stage companies.
  2. Which degree programs are a good foundation?
    • BS Data Science is the most direct fit, with machine learning, math for ML, big data, and AI courses.
    • MS Computer Science adds ML and deep learning, NLP (the same methods behind protein language models and literature mining), and AI in the cloud.
    • MS / BS Cybersecurity covers protecting IP and sensitive health and genomic data.
    • The Data Mining & Analytics graduate certificate is the fastest way in for bench scientists who want ML skills without a full degree.
    • The DIT covers leaders who are responsible for data strategy and governance.
    • The planned BS in AI, now in development, will add agentic AI and MLOps.
    • Gap: none of these has biology content. A life-sciences track would need a partner to provide the domain side, and Shenzhen Poly could be that partner (see question 4).
  3. Could CityUx help with learning outside a degree?
    • Yes. This is where we can move fastest. A short "AI for Life-Science Professionals" series could run as workshops and seminars, with micro-credentials on Open edX. Example topics: Python and ML for lab data, AI agents for literature and regulatory work, and data governance for genomic and health data.
    • Two audiences: displaced biotech workers who need to reskill, and employer cohorts through contract training.
    • Built-in pathway: credits from the short courses can stack toward the graduate certificate and then the MS, so the short courses also bring in degree students.
  4. Lessons from China, and joint work with our partner universities?
    • We already have a working channel: our partnership with Shenzhen Polytechnic University. CityU faculty teach in Shenzhen through the exchange program (Sam has taught there alongside other faculty), and Shenzhen Poly students come to our Seattle campus.
    • We have also taught the China class virtually: Clark taught it remotely during COVID. So joint work doesn't depend on travel, and a hybrid mix of in-person exchange and virtual co-teaching is something we've done before.
    • Lesson: students responded best to hands-on courses where they built something they could show. A website-building course with WordPress was a favorite in the exchange. An AI-for-biology offering should work the same way, with students building a working tool rather than sitting through theory.
    • Shenzhen Poly already has the biology side we lack. Its School of Food and Drug teaches Pharmacy, Food Biotechnology, and Food Inspection & Testing. It runs a joint industry college with Hepalink, a listed Shenzhen pharmaceutical company, and has practice bases at large firms including Sinopharm and Centre Testing International. A joint offering could pair their life-science students and industry links with our AI and data teaching.
    • Keep joint work on education and methods, not data. China restricts exporting human genetic data, and new US rules restrict sending bulk US genomic and health data to China. Joint work built on public datasets and open models avoids both problems.
    • Ideas that build on the Shenzhen Poly exchange: (a) add an applied AI-for-biology module to what our faculty already teach in Shenzhen. (b) Offer the exchange students who come to Seattle a short AI-for-biology micro-credential through CityUx, which could connect them with Seattle life-science employers. (c) Run a joint virtual seminar series comparing the US and China biotech ecosystems. Chinese biotechs are now a major source of drug candidates licensed by Western pharma, which ties directly to the panel's topic. (d) Run a co-taught project with mixed CityU–Shenzhen Poly teams working on public protein and genomics datasets.
    • Guardrail: anything involving human data or export-controlled technology goes through compliance review first.

What the panel is about, and why it touches us

Background on the panel's three threads, in case Suzanne wants more than the bullets
Capital
Seattle life-sciences venture funding is down about 58% through Q3 (per the panel description). Less money means smaller teams that have to get more done per person. AI tools and AI-skilled staff are how companies try to do that. It also means layoffs, and the people laid off are a reskilling audience.
China
The China thread is about deal terms. Western pharma increasingly licenses drug candidates from Chinese biotechs, which puts price pressure on early-stage US companies. At the same time, US biosecurity legislation and data-security rules limit work with some Chinese partners and limit where sensitive data can go. Drug-pricing policy adds pressure on what early assets are worth. For us, the relevant part is that compliance, data governance, and security are now board-level topics for small companies.
AI edge
Seattle's case for "AI-driven biology" rests on having both computational biology research (the UW Institute for Protein Design, Fred Hutch, the Allen Institute) and a deep pool of cloud and AI engineers. The bottleneck is people who can work in both worlds. STC can supply the computing half of that combination.
Philanthropy
With venture money scarce, foundations matter more. Workforce training is something foundations fund and CityU can deliver. The DeoSpheres / Microsoft funding conversations are a model for this kind of grant.

China: lessons and joint-work ideas

Built on the Shenzhen Polytechnic University partnership
Partner
Shenzhen Polytechnic University. The exchange runs both ways. CityU faculty teach in Shenzhen (Sam has, alongside other faculty), and Shenzhen Poly students come to the CityU Seattle campus.
Virtual
We've co-taught remotely. Clark taught the China class virtually during COVID. That shows the partnership can run without travel, which helps when travel or visas are hard.
Lesson
Hands-on courses land. Students in the exchange liked the website-building course with WordPress, where they built something real and could show it. Any AI-for-biology module should be built around making a working tool. (Recalled by Clark from Sam's experience; Sam can add more.)
Biology side
Shenzhen Poly's School of Food and Drug has three majors: Pharmacy, Food Biotechnology, and Food Inspection & Testing Technology. It runs a jointly built industry college with Hepalink, a pharmaceutical company listed in Shenzhen and Hong Kong. It also has off-campus practice bases at firms including Sinopharm (through its subsidiary Shenzhen Zhijun Pharmaceutical), BGI, and Centre Testing International. The school lists more than 180 practice bases in all. This is the domain expertise our programs lack. Source: SZPU School of Food and Drug page, dated Dec 2021, so the partner list should be reconfirmed.
Why Shenzhen
Shenzhen is one of China's main biotech and genomics centers. BGI, one of Shenzhen Poly's practice-base partners, is a US concern: BGI entities appear on US trade-restriction lists and in biosecurity legislation. Joint work should stay in the classroom and on public data, and should not go through BGI. The pharmacy, testing, and food-biotech partners carry less risk, but any industry piece still needs the compliance review below.
Guardrail
Methods, not data. China's human genetic resources rules restrict sending human genetic data out of China. US rules restrict sending bulk sensitive US data, including genomic and health data, to China. US biosecurity legislation targets certain Chinese biotech firms. Joint academic work should use public datasets (for example protein structure and sequence databases) and open models, and get a compliance review before anything touches human data.
Idea A
Joint seminar series: "AI for Biology, Seattle and Shenzhen." Virtual, with one CityU speaker and one Shenzhen Poly speaker per session. Covers methods and how the two ecosystems compare, including the cross-border licensing trend the panel will discuss. Low cost and low risk, and it can run on CityUx.
Idea B
Co-taught micro-credential with mixed student teams. Mixed CityU–Shenzhen Poly teams build a small project on public datasets, taught the way we taught virtually during COVID. Both universities award it, and it can stack into our graduate certificate.
Idea C
Faculty exchange on open models. Many leading open-weight models come from Chinese labs. Comparing how each side teaches with them fits what Sam and Clark discussed on Nov 9 about open models as a lower-cost option. See Nov 9 notes.
Idea D
DIT research case studies on cross-border biotech deal structures and data governance. This uses the DIT Forum and the Applied Research Symposium as places to present the work.
