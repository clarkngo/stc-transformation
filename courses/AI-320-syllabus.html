Course Description

Fundamentals of AI provides a comprehensive introduction to artificial intelligence (AI), covering both traditional and generative concepts and techniques. This course emphasizes how each family of techniques works, where it fits, and how to judge its results in real applications. Students will explore intelligent agents, search and constraint satisfaction, game playing and decision making, and the fundamentals of machine learning. The curriculum then moves to neural networks and deep learning, natural language processing, large language models, and generative AI techniques, and closes with AI ethics, risks, and responsible AI, including the societal impact of automation. Learners gain hands-on experience implementing basic AI algorithms and models in weekly programming exercises and, in a team project, comparing large language models against a traditional AI baseline. Mastery of these concepts will equip students to analyze traditional and generative AI techniques, weigh their strengths, weaknesses, and ethical implications, and design simple AI projects that combine both.

Course Outcomes

This course will prepare students to:

O1 Understand the fundamental principles of traditional AI techniques and their applications across various industries.

O2 Apply programming skills to implement basic AI algorithms and models through hands-on projects.

O3 Analyze both traditional and Generative AI techniques.

O4 Evaluate AI techniques’ strengths, weaknesses, and ethical implications in the context of applications.

O5 Design and develop a simple AI project incorporating traditional and Generative AI techniques.

Additional Information

Core Concepts & Tools

  • Foundations of AI and intelligent agents: environments, percepts, actions, and agent types

  • Search and constraint satisfaction: uninformed and informed (heuristic) search, constraint problems

  • Games and decision making: adversarial search and decision-making under uncertainty

  • Machine learning fundamentals: supervised and unsupervised learning, training and evaluation

  • Neural networks, deep learning, and an overview of computer vision

  • Natural language processing and large language models (LLMs)

  • Generative AI techniques and how they compare with traditional AI methods

  • AI ethics, risks, and responsible AI

  • Python programming in the zyBooks labs; free tiers or open-weight models for the Team Project

Team Project (TP) - Grading Criteria

Project Description: A Comparison of Large Language Models (LLMs) Against a Traditional AI Baseline

The teams can consist of 1 to 4 students. The size of the team is left up to the class instructor's judgement. The objective of the team project is to compare different Large Language Models (LLMs). Each team will select a diverse set of LLMs based on their popularity, capabilities, and availability. First, each team member will choose a different LLM, such as a GPT model (OpenAI), Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google), Llama (Meta), or Mistral (Mistral AI), using free tiers or open-weight models wherever possible. Second, the team must define evaluation criteria, such as accuracy, fluency, context understanding, speed, cost, etc. Each team member must select at least two criteria. Third, the team will conduct experiments using various datasets. The datasets should cover different tasks, such as text generation, question answering, and summarization, ensuring diversity to test the models' versatility. Finally, the team will analyze and present their findings. This will involve comparing the models' performance based on the defined criteria, highlighting each model's strengths and weaknesses, and summarizing the overall findings.

In addition, the team builds one traditional AI baseline for at least one of its tasks (for example, a TF-IDF + logistic-regression classifier, a rule-based or search-based question answerer, or an extractive summarizer) and compares it with the LLMs on the same criteria. The final report recommends when the traditional approach, the generative approach, or a combination of the two is the better fit, including its ethical implications.

Team Project Assignments

Weeks 1-3: TP01 Team Project Proposal
During the first week, the Team Project will be explained to the students. The first submission will be a project proposal and is due at the end of Week 3 using the provided template in Brightspace.

Weeks 4-7: TP02 Team Project Progress
The second submission will demonstrate progress on the team project and will be due at the end of Week 7. The students will be provided with the final report template in Brightspace, and this assignment will ask the students to complete several sections of the final report. This will be explained in week 4.

Weeks 8-10: TP03 Team Project Final Report & TP04 Team Project Presentation
The final submission will include two documents: (1) The Team Project Final Report and (2) the Team Project Presentation. Both documents are due at the end of Week 10. It will be the instructor's judgment whether the final Team Presentation is performed live or voiced over PPT slides.

Each group member, as part of the assignment for TP03 and TP04, will be asked to reflect individually on their team members' cooperation, quality of work produced, attendance at scheduled group meetings, and their contributions for the final report and presentation. These peer evaluations will only be seen by the instructor and will NOT be shared with the team members.

*All driving questions & project topics will be approved by the instructor before beginning the project.

The Muddiest Point (MP) - Grading Criteria

Before class, students are required to submit the Muddiest Point (MP) activity. The MP consists of writing a brief reflective essay identifying the most confusing part (i.e., the MP) of the content covered in the upcoming module. If a student understands all concepts, the student needs to explain the most exciting aspect. The purpose of this activity is to stimulate student engagement. The instructor uses the MP to assess how students understood the required readings. The instructor also uses the MP to customize the lecture scope to implement Just-in-Time Teaching (JiTT).

Concept Debate (CD) - Grading Criteria

The instructor poses a problem based on the key concepts of a lecture for that particular week. After reflecting on the problem, students post their first answer with justification identifying why the answer they provided is the correct answer. In the second set of postings, the students discuss their responses with their classmates. This is where the students discuss their thought processes and solutions with peers. In the third posting the student then can commit to a classmate posting to their original posting and re-submit their responses if they choose to. The instructor reviews responses and thought processes with the correct answer through Weekly Announcements.

Knowledge Check (KC) - Grading Criteria

All ten modules will have a quiz to measure the knowledge concepts acquired during each module. The questions can be true/false, multiple choice, or short answer. The quiz length will depend on the material covered during the module but can range from one to five questions per quiz. Focus on the underlying principles and concepts rather than memorization to solve the quizzes. The student will be provided two opportunities to answer the KC questions for each quiz.
