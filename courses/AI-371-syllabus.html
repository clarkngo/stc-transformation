Course Description

Cyber and AI Ethics moves beyond traditional cyber ethics to address rapidly evolving AI challenges. This course emphasizes applying established ethical frameworks and professional codes to decisions made both by people and by automated systems. Students will explore Deontology, Utilitarianism, and Virtue Ethics, professional codes of conduct, and responsible disclosure, then apply them to algorithmic bias, automated decision-making, generative AI ethics, data equity, and digital surveillance. The curriculum also covers accountability when an AI agent causes harm, AI-driven misinformation and its effect on civil liberties, the “alignment problem,” and AI governance frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the EU AI Act. Learners gain hands-on experience through case-based virtual labs, including a bias audit, a research paper on a real AI case, and a team project that analyzes an ethical failure. Mastery of these concepts will equip students to develop an Ethical AI Framework that incorporates data transparency, model explainability, and human-in-the-loop oversight.

Course Outcomes

This course will prepare students to:

O1 Explain fundamental ethical frameworks (Deontology, Utilitarianism, Virtue Ethics) and how they apply to both human cyber-behavior and AI logic.

O2 Analyze sources of algorithmic bias, data discrimination in ML pipelines, and AI-driven misinformation impacting civil liberties.

O3 Apply professional ethical codes to resolve conflicts related to privacy, security, and automated decision-making.

O4 Evaluate ethical implications of autonomous systems regarding accountability when an AI agent causes harm.

O5 Develop a comprehensive Ethical AI Framework incorporating data transparency, model explainability, and human-in-the-loop oversight.

Additional Information

Core Concepts & Tools

  • Ethical frameworks (Deontology, Utilitarianism, Virtue Ethics) applied to human cyber-behavior and to AI logic

  • Professional codes of conduct (ACM Code of Ethics, the Cybersecurity Code of Honor) and responsible disclosure, including AI incidents

  • Accountability when an AI agent causes harm: mapping data providers, developers, deployers, operators, and users

  • Algorithmic bias and data equity in ML pipelines; a no-code bias audit (selection rates, false-positive rates, the four-fifths rule)

  • Automated decision-making and human-in-the-loop oversight

  • Deepfakes and AI-driven misinformation; intellectual property and generative AI

  • Privacy, digital surveillance, and civil liberties

  • The alignment problem and AI governance (NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act)

Team Project (TP) - Grading Criteria

Project Description: Cyber and AI Ethics – Ethical AI Framework Development

Each student team will conduct an in-depth case analysis for a selected case which describes unethical behaviors related to Information Security or an AI system (for example, a biased automated decision, an AI-driven misinformation campaign, unchecked surveillance, or an AI agent acting without adequate oversight). Each case will need to be approved by the instructor before being accepted. For each case, the team will provide an analysis which describes the breakdown of ethical behaviors.

The team should differentiate between legal unethical behaviors and illegal behaviors and explain how the initial lapses led to more serious behavior. The student will describe how Information and Communications Technology (ICT) and AI impacted ethical behaviors and situations. The student will outline controls, education, and environmental factors which may have prevented ethical breaches or minimized consequences, or both.

Building on that analysis, the team will develop an Ethical AI Framework for the organization in the case. The framework must address, at minimum: data transparency (what data is used and where it comes from), model explainability (how decisions can be explained to the people they affect), human-in-the-loop oversight (where a person reviews, overrides, or stops the system), accountability (who answers when the system causes harm), and alignment with a professional code and at least one governance standard (e.g., the NIST AI RMF or the EU AI Act).

The analysis of the case should include proper documentation of the case, including news articles or articles providing supporting information. All documentation should use APA standards.

The Muddiest Point (MP) - Grading Criteria

Before class, students are required to submit the Muddiest Point (MP) activity. The MP consists of writing a brief reflective essay identifying the most confusing part (i.e., the MP) of the content covered in the upcoming module. If a student understands all concepts, the student needs to explain the most exciting aspect. The purpose of this activity is to stimulate student engagement. The instructor uses the MP to assess how students understood the required readings. The instructor also uses the MP to customize the lecture scope to implement Just-in-Time Teaching (JiTT).

Concept Debate (CD) - Grading Criteria

The instructor poses a problem based on the key concepts of a lecture. After reflecting on the problem, students submit their first answer with justification identifying why the answer is correct. Then, students discuss their responses with their classmates. Students discuss their thought processes and solutions with peers. Students then commit to an answer and re-submit their responses. The instructor reviews responses and thought processes with the correct answer through Weekly Announcements.
