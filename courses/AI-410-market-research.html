AI 410 — Comparable Courses Research

8 programs surveyed 4 categories Universities · Companies · Creators · Research + MP/CD async deep dive

What this covers

For each program: format (self-paced video, live cohort, project-based), content, target audience, technology taught, whether the course itself is free, and whether the technology stack it teaches is free to use. Organized into the four categories asked for: universities/colleges, companies, known YouTubers/creator tutorials, and articles/academic research on curriculum design. A focused deep dive follows on making Muddiest Point and Concept Debate work as graded activities in a fully asynchronous course with no live lecture.

Universities & Colleges

Stanford CS 224G — Building and Scaling LLM Applications

Course: tuition (Stanford enrollment) Tech stack: free/open tiers available In-person, project-based Requires ML background
Format: 10 weeks, 4 two-week sprints, twice-weekly in-person sessions (not recorded, no remote option). Teams of 2-5. Grading: 10% participation, 45% sprint checkpoints, 45% Demo Day.
Content/Technology: Reasoning models (o1/o3/R1), multi-agent systems, RAG 2.0, context engineering, production deployment & observability, voice AI, AI safety/ethics.
Target audience: Stanford students with Python (CS106B) and ML fundamentals (CS221/CS229) already under their belt, or instructor-permission entry with substantial practical LLM experience — a notably higher bar than AI 410's CS352/DS479 prerequisites.
Relevant to AI 410: The closest structural peer to our "one running app extended all term" philosophy — but assumes far more prior ML depth, and its all-in-person, unrecorded format is the opposite of the Codespaces-anywhere approach AI 410 is built around.

UC Berkeley — Large Language Model Agents (course & public MOOC)

MOOC version: free Enrolled version: tuition Lecture-based + MOOC Grad/advanced undergrad
Format: Runs as a for-credit Berkeley course with a parallel, publicly-available MOOC version (recorded lectures, no live component required).
Content/Technology: Reasoning strategies, RAG, benchmarking, safety and ethics, multi-agent collaboration — broader theoretical/research framing than AI 410's build-first approach.
Target audience: Graduate-level framing; the public MOOC lowers the bar but still assumes comfort with reading research papers alongside the lecture content.
Relevant to AI 410: Confirms the free-MOOC-alongside-paid-enrollment pattern is common at the university level — something to consider if AI 410 material is ever offered as an open resource beyond CityU's own students.

Companies

DeepLearning.AI — Short Courses (RAG, Agentic RAG, AI Agents in LangGraph)

First module free, rest paid Tech stack: free tiers Self-paced video Intermediate Python
Format: The RAG course: 49 video lessons, 9 code examples, 10 graded assignments, ~26 hours over ~1 month at 5 hrs/week. Individual short courses (Agentic RAG w/ LlamaIndex, AI Agents in LangGraph) run under 2 hours each.
Content/Technology: Vector databases (Weaviate), keyword/semantic/hybrid search, chunking, query parsing, monitoring (Arize Phoenix), open-source LLMs via Together AI — applied across e-commerce, media, healthcare domains.
Target audience: Intermediate Python required; basic gen-AI and high-school math "helpful," not required.
Relevant to AI 410: The freemium model (one free module, pay for the rest + certificate) is a real alternative to AI 410's fully-included structure — worth noting if cost to students ever becomes a factor in tooling choices (we chose Chroma/Gemini free tiers specifically to avoid this tension).

Google Cloud — "Agentic AI on Google Cloud" Learning Path

Course: free Tech stack: free credits, paid beyond Self-paced labs Broad / cert-seekers
Format: 8 self-paced activities, hands-on labs against real (sandboxed) Google Cloud instances — no personal paid subscription needed for the labs themselves. Ends in a Google Cloud skill badge/certificate.
Content/Technology: Foundational agent architecture through enterprise multi-agent deployment, on Google's own agent-building stack (Vertex AI-adjacent tooling).
Target audience: Broad — framed as accessible to anyone pursuing a recognized credential, not gated by a formal prerequisite course.
Relevant to AI 410: A cloud vendor's course is inherently a funnel toward its own paid platform post-certification — a reminder that AI 410's choice of vendor-neutral, genuinely-free tools (Gemini free tier, Chroma, Upstash Redis free tier) is a deliberate design choice, not a default.

Data Science Dojo — Agentic AI Bootcamp

Paid bootcamp Tech stack: free/open tiers 10-week cohort ML/backend engineers
Format: 10 weeks — the closest length/topic-sequence match to AI 410's own schedule (vector DBs, RAG, agent patterns, protocols, multi-agent capstone in week 10).
Content/Technology: Vector databases, RAG, agentic design patterns, MCP/A2A protocols.
Target audience: Prerequisites of "basic Python" plus prior LLM/transformer/embeddings knowledge — targets ML engineers, backend engineers, and solutions architects, a technical audience AI 410 deliberately doesn't assume.
Relevant to AI 410: Already informed AI 410's schedule length and topic ordering (see AI-410.html's own research section) — its pacing (3-4 new concepts per week) is exactly what AI 410's scaffolding avoids.

Known Creators & Company-Produced Tutorials

freeCodeCamp.org (YouTube) — Agentic AI / Production RAG courses

Fully free Tech stack: free tiers Long-form video (8-24 hrs) Self-directed beginners+
Format: Single long-form YouTube videos rather than a structured multi-week program — a 24-hour "Agentic AI using LangGraph" course and an 8-hour "Production RAG with LangChain & Vector Databases" course, both build-along.
Content/Technology: LangChain, LangGraph (stateful cyclical agent workflows), Human-in-the-Loop, RAG, streaming responses, vector database optimization, observability.
Target audience: Self-directed learners; no formal prerequisite beyond willingness to follow a long build-along video, though the pace assumes comfort reading code quickly.
Relevant to AI 410: The "watch one giant video and type along" format has zero structural scaffolding, checkpoints, or feedback loop — the opposite end of the spectrum from AI 410's weekly HOS + Concept Debate + Knowledge Check structure. Useful as a contrast case for why structure matters for a beginner-leaning cohort specifically.

Hugging Face — AI Agents Course

Fully free, incl. certificate Tech stack: free tiers Self-paced units Beginner (basic Python)
Format: Unit 0 (onboarding) through Unit 4 (final project), plus 3 bonus units, run on pre-configured Hugging Face Spaces (no local setup). ~3-4 hrs/week, 4-6 weeks, no deadline pressure. Certificate requires Unit 1 + one use-case assignment + the final challenge.
Content/Technology: Three real frameworks — smolagents, LlamaIndex, and LangGraph — plus the Thought-Action-Observation loop, tool calling, agent observability/evaluation, and (bonus) fine-tuning for function-calling.
Target audience: Explicitly beginner-friendly — "basic Python" and "a rough idea of what an LLM does," with Unit 1 including a recap for anyone shakier than that.
Relevant to AI 410: The strongest match so far to AI 410's own "beginner-friendly but genuinely hands-on" positioning — runnable notebooks in a pre-configured environment mirrors AI 410's Codespaces-first approach, and the leaderboard/challenge format is a lightweight gamification idea AI 410 doesn't currently use.

Articles & Academic Research on Curriculum Design

Rausch, Wittek, Geger & Inkermann — "Preparing Students for AI-Driven Agile Development: A Project-Based AI Engineering Curriculum"

Open-access paper Academic research CS educators
Argument: Traditional lecture-heavy AI courses and software-engineering courses stay siloed, leaving students prepared for a "workplace without AI." Proposes a project-based curriculum (from TU Clausthal's Digital Technologies program) where students build AI-integrated applications inside real, repeated agile project settings, with continuous teamwork and feedback rather than one-off assignments.
Relevant to AI 410: Directly validates AI 410's "one running app all term" design principle over the more common "ten disposable projects" pattern seen in several other programs above — this paper is making the same argument from the software-engineering-education research side.

"Teaching Software Engineering with LLM and MCP Integration: From Classroom to Industry Practice"

Open-access paper Academic research CS educators
Argument: Identifies a gap in teaching resources specifically for Model Context Protocol (MCP) and LLM-integrated software engineering — most existing studies lack a systematic, industry-oriented teaching design for this material.
Relevant to AI 410: A live gap AI 410 doesn't currently address either — MCP isn't part of the course's tool-calling coverage (it uses direct function-calling instead). Worth a note for a future revision if MCP becomes more standard in the field before the next redesign.

Deep Dive: Muddiest Point & Concept Debate Without a Live Lecture

Why this needed its own research pass

Both techniques were originally designed around a live class session — Muddiest Point (MP) is classically submitted so the instructor can address it in the next lecture (the core move of Just-in-Time Teaching), and Concept Debate (CD) is often run as live back-and-forth. AI 410 is fully asynchronous — no live virtual lecture — yet the Dean requires both as graded activities. The question: do these techniques still work without the live session they were built around, and if so, how should they be structured?

Muddiest Point → adapted for async (the JiTT substitute)

Classroom assessment technique Any level
Finding: MP adapts cleanly to async delivery via an end-of-week survey or discussion-board post rather than an in-class index card. It works because the mechanism that matters isn't the live moment — it's normalizing confusion (asking all students to respond independently, without the social pressure of admitting confusion in front of peers) and giving the instructor timely, specific information to act on.
The live-lecture gap, and how JiTT research handles it: Just-in-Time Teaching's core move — reading responses right before class and adjusting the lecture on the spot — has no direct equivalent without a live session. The adaptation literature substitutes three things: a structured timeline (regular deadlines so responses arrive before the next content unlocks), frequent short assessments rather than one big one, and iterative adjustment of the asynchronous materials themselves (updating a recorded clip, a written FAQ, or the next module) instead of a live pivot.
Effectiveness note: MP interventions are more effective with frequent, repeated use — students get better at asking specific, useful questions as the pattern becomes familiar, which argues for doing it every week rather than occasionally.
Sources: ASU Learning & Teaching Hub — Muddiest Points, Penn State — Muddiest Point, MVCC — Just-in-Time Teaching, MSU Denver — JiTT in Canvas.

Mitchell (2019) — "Using Debate in an Online Asynchronous Social Policy Course"

Peer-reviewed, open access N=36, quant + qual Graduate students
Structure used: not a single discussion-board thread — a staged, semester-long assignment: individual position statement (week 1) → group position statement (week 3) → written rebuttal (week 6) → open asynchronous discussion forum (week 8) → reflection paper (week 9). Students were randomly assigned pro/con, then worked in groups.
Quantitative results (% who agreed or strongly agreed): helped understand course concepts 80%; understood concepts better than lecture/reading would have 78%; helped critical thinking 92%; improved collaboration 92%; helped in other courses/work 57% — but only 28% said it made them feel more engaged in the course.
Qualitative themes: students reported real apprehension going in — nervousness about arguing, about group work with people they'd never meet in person, about the format being unfamiliar — but this largely resolved once the activity was underway. A recurring theme was students being forced to argue a position they didn't personally hold, which "challenged their own viewpoint" more than a same-side discussion would have.
Relevant to AI 410: The 80/28 split is the single most important number here — structured async debate is strong for understanding and critical thinking, weak for engagement on its own. AI 410's Concept Debate shouldn't be asked to carry the course's engagement load; that's what the CEAU build artifacts and Knowledge Checks are for. The staged structure (individual → group → rebuttal → open forum) is directly adoptable, scaled down from a semester to a single week.

General async discussion-board effectiveness — cross-study findings

Multiple studies Higher ed, general
What consistently predicts quality: active instructor participation and feedback in the thread — students explicitly associate discussion quality with the instructor showing up, not just posting a prompt and grading silently later. Clear structure and expectations (what counts as a response, minimum posts, deadlines) and a designed question prompt matter more than the platform.
Social presence: discussions work better when students can build some sense of who they're talking to — varying group size (not always whole-class) gives more students room to lead a discussion rather than lurk.
Relevant to AI 410: If MP and CD are now graded, whoever teaches the course needs to budget real time to actually read and respond in those threads — the research is fairly blunt that ungraded-by-the-instructor-in-practice discussion boards underperform, regardless of how well the assignment itself is designed.

"Bringing Interactivity to Fully Asynchronous Programs"

Program design case study Adult/professional learners
Approach: a fully self-paced, no-live-session program that still needed to "mimic important shared and active learning components of facilitated sessions." Solution: small cohorts (~10 people) for a sense of shared progress, automated weekly pacing nudges, short post-tests at each unit, a cohort-scoped discussion board, and reflective free-text exercises.
Relevant to AI 410: The cohort-scoped discussion board (not one giant whole-class thread) is worth considering for CD specifically — smaller groups likely produce more genuine back-and-forth than a 30+ student thread, echoing the "vary group size" finding above.

Concrete design for AI 410's MP & CD, given no live lecture

Muddiest Point: a short async submission (form or discussion post) due before that week's HOS build begins, based on the week's recorded/written lecture material. The JiTT "pivot" becomes a short instructor-posted response — text or a short recorded clip — addressing the 2-3 most common muddiest points, published early in the week alongside or right after the Concept Debate opens. Concept Debate: stage it rather than leave it as one freeform thread — an initial position, a required response to at least one peer, and (mirroring Mitchell's design) run it in smaller groups rather than one whole-class thread. Don't lean on CD for engagement — Mitchell's own data says it won't carry that weight; the CEAU build and Knowledge Check are where engagement should come from. Budget instructor time — the research is consistent that visible instructor participation in these threads is what actually drives quality, not the assignment design alone.

What This Means for AI 410

Validated by this research

The "one running app all term" principle (vs. many disposable projects) has direct academic backing (Rausch et al.). Building entirely on free-tier tooling (Gemini, Chroma, Upstash Redis, Langfuse, Expo) is genuinely uncommon — most programs surveyed either charge for the course itself or fund labs via a vendor's own paid platform. AI 410's beginner-friendly-but-hands-on positioning has real precedent in Hugging Face's Agents Course specifically, including the pre-configured-environment approach.

Gaps or ideas worth considering

No program surveyed uses a lightweight gamification layer (Hugging Face's leaderboard/challenge) — could be a low-cost addition to a HOS or Knowledge Check. MCP (Model Context Protocol) is a live gap in both AI 410 and the wider teaching literature — worth watching for the next redesign rather than adding now. Every free program still gates the *certificate* behind some paid or institutional step (Coursera enrollment, university credit) — AI 410 doesn't have this tension since it's already inside a degree program, but it's a reminder that "free" rarely means free-with-credential elsewhere.
