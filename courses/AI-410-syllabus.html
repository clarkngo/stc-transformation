Course Description

AI Engineering applies rigorous software engineering principles to the development and deployment of autonomous, learning-based systems. This course emphasizes leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) within purpose-built software solutions to address real-world business challenges. Students will explore system architecture for agent development and integration, context engineering through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and vector databases, and best practices for deploying and monitoring AI models in production environments. The curriculum also covers probabilistic logic for handling nondeterministic outputs. Learners gain hands-on experience building a full-stack AI solution, focusing on asynchronous data processing, push-based AI notifications, and seamless integration of vector stores for dynamic RAG applications. Mastery of these concepts will equip students to design and implement robust AI-powered applications using full-stack AI.

Course Outcomes

This course will prepare students to:

O1 Define and explain the essential components of full-stack AI systems, such as LLM architecture, agentic loops, and the differences between prompt engineering and context engineering.

O2 Design and implement a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline using vector databases to deliver context to autonomous agents.

O3 Analyze and break down the multi-layered architecture of agent-driven applications to identify and address potential bottlenecks in asynchronous data processing and nondeterministic reasoning.

O4 Evaluate and critique the performance and reliability of production AI models using appropriate observability tools and metrics for assessing probabilistic outputs.

O5 Develop a fully functional, agent-driven mobile application that seamlessly integrates real-time notifications and persistent vector storage into a comprehensive full-stack solution.

Additional Information

Core Concepts & Tools

  • Full-stack AI system architecture: a frontend client, a backend API holding the model credentials, and an LLM provider, communicating over HTTP

  • LLM architecture and the agentic loop: structured tool calling, multi-turn reasoning, and the difference between prompt engineering and context engineering

  • Context engineering via Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): chunking, embeddings, vector database storage and similarity search (Chroma)

  • Probabilistic logic and guardrails: validating and retrying against nondeterministic model output

  • Asynchronous data processing: background job queues (Redis/RQ) and status polling

  • Mobile clients and push-based AI notifications (React Native / Expo)

  • Observability and evaluation of production AI systems: request tracing and faithfulness scoring (Langfuse)

  • Deployment: containerizing and deploying the API, worker, and vector store to a live host

Hands-On Skill (HOS) - Grading Criteria

HOS is designed to support “learning-by-example” and “learning-by-doing.” The instructor will assign HOS exercises to a pair of students in class or individually online. Students experience how to create a system by reorganizing elements into a new pattern or structure through system development or virtual labs.    

HOS has two graded categories. Engagement is the AI-assisted starting point for scaffolding, not the deliverable. Skills are emphasized in each HOS to let students learn how to evaluate, analyze, apply, and understand the given system.

Each HOS is done in the student's own GitHub Classroom repository for that module, in GitHub Codespaces. Written work for each stage goes in the repository's hos/NOTES.md, and the student submits the repository link to that HOS's Brightspace assignment at the end of the second week. Commit history shows when each stage was completed.

  • HOS 1 — Foundations & the Agentic Loop

Students use an AI coding assistant in GitHub Codespaces to build a full-stack chat application: a FastAPI backend that calls the Gemini API, and a Streamlit frontend. They extend it with an agentic loop, in which the model calls a real tool through function calling and the result goes back to the model before it replies. Students then test whether the AI-generated code actually behaves correctly, explain in their own words how the loop decides to call a tool, and add a second tool by hand without AI assistance.

  • HOS 2 — Embeddings, Chunking & RAG

Students add retrieval-augmented generation to their HOS 1 application. They chunk a set of documents, embed them with Gemini’s embedding model, store them in a Chroma vector database, and ground the agent’s answers in the retrieved passages. Students test three things: whether answers use the documents, whether tool calling still works alongside retrieval, and whether the agent admits when the documents don’t cover a question. They explain why documents and queries are embedded differently, then write and ingest a new document by hand.

  • HOS 3 — Guardrails & Async Processing

Students make the application reliable under two kinds of unpredictability. First, they wrap tool calls in a validation guardrail (Pydantic) that returns errors to the model and retries. Second, they move slow document ingestion into a background job queue (Redis, free tier) so the application stays responsive. Students test malformed tool calls and a stopped worker, explain the specific failure each design choice prevents, and extend the guardrail to a second tool by hand.

  • HOS 4 — Mobile, Push & Observability

Students bring the application to production shape with three additions:

  • a mobile client (Expo / React Native)

  • push notifications when background work finishes

  • Langfuse tracing, so every request shows retrieval and generation as separate steps with their own latency and token use

Students confirm that a notification actually arrives on a device and read traces to judge what the agent did. By hand, they write a score that measures whether answers are grounded in what was retrieved.

  • AC 1 — Foundations & the Agentic Loop

Students build the tools for a fictional clinic’s scheduling assistant (find open slots, book, cancel), plus the tool definitions the model sees, and must pass the provided tests. The open decision is what the assistant does when a patient cancels within 24 hours of the appointment, when a $40 late fee applies. Students also decide whether two more requested tools, viewing patient records and sending text messages, should be available to the model at all.

  • AC 2 — Embeddings, Chunking & RAG

Students measure an unfamiliar college help-desk retrieval pipeline against a set of questions with known answers, then beat it with their own chunking strategy. The open decision is the match-score threshold below which the bot should say “I don’t know” rather than risk a wrong answer. Students also sort the remaining misses by cause: how the text was chunked, or a mismatch between the student’s words and the article’s.

  • AC 3 — Guardrails & Async Processing

Students build the guardrail for a coffee retailer’s refund tool: a validation schema, checks against order records, and a human review queue. They then run a set of recorded faulty model calls through it. The open decision is which refunds the bot may issue without a person, below a fixed $500 limit. Students also explain which layer stops each faulty call: the schema, the order lookup, or human review.

  • AC 4 — Mobile, Push & Observability

Students add observability to a grocery delivery service’s AI substitution feature: traces, steps, and a quality score for each substituted item. They run a recorded day of orders through it. The open decision is which failures should page an on-call engineer immediately and which belong on a dashboard. Students also explain what each traced step shows that a single log line would not.

Project Description

Teams of three students (four with instructor approval) design, build, and present one agent-driven, full-stack AI application that solves a realistic business problem (O5). The system combines all four HOS:

  • an agentic loop with tool calling (HOS 1)

  • retrieval over a vector store (HOS 2)

  • guardrails and background processing (HOS 3)

  • a mobile client with push notifications and observability (HOS 4)

It also applies the judgment practiced in the Application Challenges, such as which actions the model may take on its own and when the system should hand off to a person.

Each team consists of three students. If necessary, a team can be made up of four students with the instructor’s approval. Each team’s role and responsibilities must be clearly described in your team’s final report.

Report

The student will provide a report formatted based on a template provided by the instructor. Students are required to improve their writing iteratively and incrementally every week. The revision will always happen during a quarter. Students will add new required sections to the existing paper every week.

The final report is the culmination of applied research and activities conducted throughout the quarter. The final report provides a detailed problem, and its solution is likely to be encountered by a company or organization described in a case study supplied by the student. 

The paper is to be between 5 and 6 pages. The required template for class submissions comes from an international organization, ISCAP (Information Systems & Computing Academic Professionals). The instructor may recommend teams submit their paper to conferences. Submissions are optional and will not impact on the course grade. Additional revisions may be required after the course.    

The file name consists of the project phase, report type, team number, and the list of your team members. Each report must be maintained in OneDrive to trace team collaboration. Each report must include the team’s GitHub repository named after keywords. Also, in the final report, the YouTube link for the demo must be included. Three report submissions are required according to the following schedule:  

  • Proposal (1 page; 10 points)

    • Duration: 3 weeks (Week 1 – Week 3)

As in any scholarly writing, students should not merely copy information from another author. Students should use evidence to support the contentions they have drawn from their findings and critically analyze related literature. In essence, each paper needs to be an analytical paper, not a summary of readings.  

Students are expected to use the assigned readings, videos, and other materials throughout the quarter. Students will need to utilize additional sources that were not assigned by the professor. While stylized after an industry report, nonetheless, students are expected to employ APA formatting of citations, footnotes, and bibliography. Students must cite the sources of all ideas, facts, and information used that are not their own, even if they have put the information into their own words. Failure to do so is plagiarism, although the oversight is unintentional. To avoid plagiarism, check https://library.cityu.edu/howto/apa-writing/avoid-plagiarism/.

The Muddiest Point (MP) - Grading Criteria

Before starting each module, students are required to submit the Muddiest Point (MP) activity. The purpose of this activity is to stimulate student engagement. The instructor uses the MP to assess how students understood each module’s HOS and its required readings. The instructor also uses the MP to customize the lecture scope to implement Just-in-Time Teaching (JiTT).

The MP consists of four short fields:

  1. What you were doing right before you got confused (the specific concept, video timestamp, or line of the guide).

  2. The exact place you got stuck — not "I don't get RAG," but "I don't understand why the query gets embedded with a different task type than the documents."

  3. What you already tried — re-reading, a specific search, asking a classmate — before submitting.

  4. Optional: your best guess at the answer, even if you think it's wrong.

Concept Debate (CD) – Grading Criteria

Students engage in a structured discussion of their reasoning with classmates. Each HOS comes with a Concept Debate question, already provided in the course, rooted in that HOS’s key concepts. Each question has two defensible sides, and students consider both before reaching a position of their own.

Before the first debate, every student joins one of two debate groups. The instructor can assign the groups, or students can choose their own, as long as the two groups stay close to even in size. The groups take turns arguing Side A and Side B from one debate to the next.

In the first week of each HOS, students complete a three-stage response sequence:

  1. Initial position (assigned side): Present a clear argument for the side your debate group is assigned, using a specific claim and supporting evidence rather than a general summary.

  2. Argue the opposite side: Before responding to a classmate, post the strongest argument for the opposing side, the position you did not initially hold. Because everyone engages with both perspectives, “I agree with you” is not a valid response.

  3. Synthesis: Set aside advocacy and state a genuine synthesis or consensus position. Reference at least one specific point from a classmate’s post, and avoid generic responses like “great points, I agree.”

Knowledge Check (KC)

Weekly quizzes assess your understanding of key concepts learned through HOSs. Emphasize grasping underlying principles over rote memorization when preparing for the quizzes. Each question has a 90-second time limit, simulating the pace of industry certification exams.

Students are required to adhere to the honor code, which strictly prohibits the use of AI assistants. You may retake each exam as many times as needed, and a passing score is set at 80%. After every attempt, you will receive feedback on any questions answered incorrectly to help guide your learning.

Antidiscrimination

City University of Seattle and its staff and faculty are committed to supporting our students. We value equity, diversity, and inclusion as a way of life as well as the educational opportunities it provides. City U will not tolerate any form of discrimination based on race, color, ethnicity, sexual orientation, gender identification, socioeconomic status, or religious values. If you have experienced any discrimination based on any of the above, we encourage you to report this to the University. Please report this to your instructor. If you do not feel safe reporting this to your instructor, please report to the Provost or to the Vice President of Student Affairs.

Non-Discrimination & Prohibition of Sexual Harassment

City University of Seattle adheres to all federal, state, and local civil rights laws prohibiting discrimination in employment and education. The University is committed to ensuring that the education environment is bounded by standards of mutual respect and safety and is free from discriminatory practices.

In the U.S., the University is required by Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 to ensure that all of its education programs and activities do not discriminate on the basis of sex/gender. Sex include sex, sex stereotypes, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, and pregnancy or parenting status. Sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, and stalking are forms of sex discrimination, which are prohibited under Title IX and by City University of Seattle policy. City University of Seattle also prohibits retaliation against any person opposing discrimination or participating in any discrimination investigation or complaint process internal or external to the institution. Questions regarding Title IX, including its application and/or concerns about noncompliance, should be directed at Title IX Coordinator. For a complete copy of the policy or for more information, visit the CityU website or contact the Title IX Coordinator.

In Canada, in compliance with the British Columbia Human Rights Code, the Alberta Human Rights Act, WorksafeBC, and the Workers’ Compensation Board of Alberta, the University believes that its environment should at all times be supportive and respectful of the dignity and self-esteem of individuals. Discrimination, harassment and bullying conduct, whether through person-to-person behavior or via electronic communications such as email or social media is not acceptable and will not be tolerated. As an educational institution, it is our responsibility to cultivate an environment of excellence, equity, mutual respect and to recognize the value and potential of every individual. The University will take all necessary steps to meet or exceed the requirements of the law to prevent discrimination, harassment and bullying. The Respectful Workplace Policy for the prevention of discrimination, harassment and bullying policy and procedure can be found at CityU in Canada website.
