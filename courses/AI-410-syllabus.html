Course Description

AI Engineering applies rigorous software engineering principles to the development and deployment of autonomous, learning-based systems. This course emphasizes leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) within purpose-built software solutions to address real-world business challenges. Students will explore system architecture for agent development and integration, context engineering through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and vector databases, and best practices for deploying and monitoring AI models in production environments. The curriculum also covers probabilistic logic for handling nondeterministic outputs. Learners gain hands-on experience building a full-stack AI solution, focusing on asynchronous data processing, push-based AI notifications, and seamless integration of vector stores for dynamic RAG applications. Mastery of these concepts will equip students to design and implement robust AI-powered applications using full-stack AI.

Course Outcomes

As a result of this course, students will know or be able to do the following:

  1. O1 — Define and explain the essential components of full-stack AI systems, such as LLM architecture, agentic loops, and the differences between prompt engineering and context engineering.
  2. O2 — Design and implement a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline using vector databases to deliver context to autonomous agents.
  3. O3 — Analyze and break down the multi-layered architecture of agent-driven applications to identify and address potential bottlenecks in asynchronous data processing and nondeterministic reasoning.
  4. O4 — Evaluate and critique the performance and reliability of production AI models using appropriate observability tools and metrics for assessing probabilistic outputs.
  5. O5 — Develop a fully functional, agent-driven mobile application that seamlessly integrates real-time notifications and persistent vector storage into a comprehensive full-stack solution.

Core Concepts & Tools

  • Full-stack AI system architecture: a frontend client, a backend API holding the model credentials, and an LLM provider, communicating over HTTP
  • LLM architecture and the agentic loop: structured tool calling, multi-turn reasoning, and the difference between prompt engineering and context engineering
  • Context engineering via Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): chunking, embeddings, vector database storage and similarity search (Chroma)
  • Probabilistic logic and guardrails: validating and retrying against nondeterministic model output
  • Asynchronous data processing: background job queues (Redis/RQ) and status polling
  • Mobile clients and push-based AI notifications (React Native / Expo)
  • Observability and evaluation of production AI systems: request tracing and faithfulness scoring (Langfuse)
  • Deployment: containerizing and deploying the API, worker, and vector store to a live host

Course Assignments and Grading

Five graded categories. Create/Scaffold is never graded on its own — across every HOS, it's the AI-assisted starting point, not the deliverable. Per the Dean's requirement, Muddiest Point and Concept Debate are graded activities in their own right, not folded into the HOS artifact. Application Challenge is a separate category from the Team/Term Project — a smaller individual transfer task right after each HOS, not the capstone.

Suggested MP format — a structured, standing submission (not a blank box)

The most common failure mode for an async Muddiest Point is students posting a low-effort, generic line ("this week was confusing") just to get participation credit. Rather than one form due at the end of the week, adopt the model used by ACQUIRED (2025) — a standing, always-open submission point available as soon as that week's material is posted, due before the HOS build begins — and require four short fields instead of a free-text box:

  1. What you were doing right before you got confused (the specific concept, video timestamp, or line of the guide).
  2. The exact place you got stuck — not "I don't get RAG," but "I don't understand why the query gets embedded with a different task_type than the documents."
  3. What you already tried — re-reading, a specific search, asking a classmate — before submitting.
  4. Optional: your best guess at the answer, even if you think it's wrong.

Requiring context + a specific point + a prior attempt makes a one-line non-answer structurally harder to submit than an actual muddiest point — and gives the instructor something concrete to respond to. ACQUIRED's own student feedback ranked "ease of asking questions" and the modular, organized format above the MP prompt itself, suggesting structure — not stricter policing — is what improves submission quality.

Suggested CD format — argue both sides, then synthesize (not "pick a side and defend it")

A freeform "state your position, respond to a peer" debate tends toward one of two failure modes: there's an obviously-correct answer, so responses collapse into "I agree" with no real engagement, or the debate has no real stakes so nobody pushes back. Mitchell (2019) found that assigning a position (not letting students choose) drove real engagement precisely because students had to argue something they didn't personally hold — and Structured Academic Controversy adds a further structural fix directly aimed at the "devolves into I Agree" problem: require students to argue both sides before any consensus is allowed. A 3-stage sequence within the week:

  1. Initial position (assigned side): argue the side you were assigned, with a specific claim and evidence — not a general summary of the topic.
  2. Argue the opposite side: before responding to any classmate, students must post the strongest version of the other side's argument — the one they didn't hold. This is the step that prevents "I agree with you" from being a viable response, since everyone has now argued both sides.
  3. Synthesis: drop advocacy entirely and state a genuine synthesis or consensus position, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post (not just "great points, I agree").

Grading rewards engagement with a specific argument someone else made — a response that only affirms agreement without citing what it's agreeing with doesn't meet the Writing criterion above.

Team/Term Project — Weekly Milestones (Weeks 7–9)

Three weeks of open project time is easy to under-plan. Each week has a specific checkpoint students should be able to show, whether asked for formally or not:

WeekMilestone students should be hitting
7Scope is locked. Which of the 4 HOS your project actually combines, and why — in writing, not just discussed verbally. If it's a team, roles/ownership per HOS piece are assigned. A rough architecture sketch exists, even hand-drawn.
8A rough, working version exists. Not polished — but something a partner or the instructor could actually run and interact with, touching at least two of the combined HOS pieces end to end. This is the week to surface integration problems, not week 9.
9Feature-complete and rehearsed. All combined HOS pieces work together; remaining time goes to polish, edge cases, and a practiced walkthrough of the Week 10 demo — not new features.
