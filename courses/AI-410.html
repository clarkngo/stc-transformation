Course Description: AI Engineering applies rigorous software engineering principles to the development and deployment of autonomous, learning-based systems. This course emphasizes leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) within purpose-built software solutions to address real-world business challenges. Students will explore system architecture for agent development and integration, context engineering through Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and vector databases, and best practices for deploying and monitoring AI models in production environments. The curriculum also covers probabilistic logic for handling nondeterministic outputs. Learners gain hands-on experience building a full-stack AI solution, focusing on asynchronous data processing, push-based AI notifications, and seamless integration of vector stores for dynamic RAG applications. Mastery of these concepts will equip students to design and implement robust AI-powered applications using full-stack AI.

Course Outcomes

As a result of this course, students will know or be able to do the following:

  1. O1 — Define and explain the essential components of full-stack AI systems, such as LLM architecture, agentic loops, and the differences between prompt engineering and context engineering.
  2. O2 — Design and implement a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline using vector databases to deliver context to autonomous agents.
  3. O3 — Analyze and break down the multi-layered architecture of agent-driven applications to identify and address potential bottlenecks in asynchronous data processing and nondeterministic reasoning.
  4. O4 — Evaluate and critique the performance and reliability of production AI models using appropriate observability tools and metrics for assessing probabilistic outputs.
  5. O5 — Develop a fully functional, agent-driven mobile application that seamlessly integrates real-time notifications and persistent vector storage into a comprehensive full-stack solution.

Course Design Philosophy: Merge, Don't Reduce

Each Hands-On Skill (HOS) unit pairs a new AI/agent concept with a new piece of backend tooling (FastAPI, Chroma, Redis/RQ, Expo, Langfuse). Asking students new to production software to absorb a new concept and a new tech component in the same sitting, eight times in a row, is cognitive overload on two axes at once — so two related skills are taught together as one merged HOS, without doubling the load.

The fix isn't cutting content — it's changing what AI does in the loop

Every one of the original 8 skills still gets taught, and all 5 Outcomes still get covered — nothing is dropped. What changes is that students use an AI coding assistant to scaffold the boilerplate (the FastAPI wiring, the Chroma setup, the Redis connection) instead of spending class time building it line by line. That reclaims enough time to teach two related skills together as one merged unit — because the part that used to eat a whole week (getting the plumbing working) now takes an afternoon.

The CEAU Cycle — Replacing "Score the Code"

If an AI assistant can produce working code, grading whether the code works stops measuring whether the student understands it. Traditional Bloom's builds up to Create as the hardest, final stage. In an AI-assisted course that ordering is backwards — Create is now the fast, cheap part. The stages that actually take skill come after it:

Traditional Bloom's OrderAI-Era Reordered Stage
Stage 1: FoundationalCreate / Scaffold
Stage 2: Critical GateEvaluate
Stage 3: Deep DissectionAnalyze
Stage 4: MasteryUnderstand & Refine

Every HOS is graded across all four stages instead of on code correctness alone:

1

Create / Scaffold

Student uses an AI assistant to get a working version running fast. Not graded on its own — this is the starting point, not the deliverable.

2

Evaluate

Is the AI-produced result actually correct? What's missing, wrong, or fragile? The critical gate — can the student judge output they didn't write by hand?

3

Analyze

Why does this work, mechanically? Dissect one specific piece (a function, a query, a request flow) and explain it in the student's own words.

4

Understand & Refine

Extend or fix something in the scaffold by hand — the mastery check. Can they change it correctly without AI doing that part too?

Each HOS's artifact is a screenshot of the working result plus a short written response walking through Evaluate → Analyze → Understand & Refine for that unit's build. Cheap to grade at class scale, and hard to fake without having actually engaged with the material.

Naming — one decided, one still open

Decided: the renamed PE — a smaller, individual transfer task after each HOS — is called "Application Challenge" — "Challenge" signals a higher Bloom's tier (evaluating, synthesizing, creating) without sounding intimidating. It's a separate category from the Team/Term Project. Still open: "the CEAU Cycle" for the per-HOS rubric structure remains a working name pending sign-off.

Weekly Breakdown

Week 1 — HOS 1: Foundations & the Agentic Loop

Merges legacy Weeks 1–2.

LLM architecture at the level a full-stack engineer needs, plus the agentic loop and tool calling — the two concepts that make everything after this possible. Students scaffold both with an AI assistant, then work through Evaluate/Analyze/Understand & Refine on the result.

Outcome 1 Open hands-on guide →
New words this week
Frontend & backend —
the two halves of the app: what the user sees (frontend) versus the server holding your API key (backend).
LLM —
the AI model itself (here, Google's Gemini) that generates the reply.
Agentic loop & tool calling —
the cycle where the model decides to use a real function instead of just replying with text, and the result comes back before it answers.
Application Challenge

Design the tool schemas (not full implementations) a completely different kind of business would need for its own agent, and justify each one — practicing the judgment call of "should the model be able to call this," not just the mechanics of calling it.

Knowledge check
  • What's the difference between the frontend and backend, and why does the API key live in the backend only?
  • Walk through what happens, step by step, from typing a message to the agent deciding to call a tool.

Week 2 — HOS 2: Embeddings, Chunking & RAG Pipelines

Merges legacy Weeks 3–4.

Context engineering end to end: chunking and embedding documents, then wiring retrieval into the agent so answers are grounded in real data instead of guessed.

Outcome 2 Open hands-on guide →
New words this week
Embedding & chunking —
splitting a document into pieces (chunking), then turning each piece into a list of numbers representing its meaning (embedding).
Vector database —
a database built to store embeddings and quickly find the closest matches to a query.
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) —
having the model answer using retrieved chunks of real text instead of only its own memory.
Application Challenge

Given a different, unfamiliar RAG pipeline's code, evaluate it: identify one thing it gets right, one thing that's likely to quietly underperform (not crash — just answer worse), and explain how you'd confirm your suspicion without running it.

Knowledge check
  • Why chunk a document before embedding it?
  • Where do retrieved chunks go before the model generates its answer, and how would you prove retrieval is actually helping?

Week 3 — HOS 3: Guardrails & Asynchronous Processing

Merges legacy Weeks 6–7.

Handling nondeterministic output (validation/retry guardrails) and moving long-running work off the request path (background jobs) — the two reliability concerns that show up together in any real production agent.

Outcome 3 Open hands-on guide →
New words this week
Nondeterministic & guardrail —
the same input can produce a different output each time (nondeterministic); a guardrail checks that output and retries or fixes it if something's wrong.
Asynchronous (async), queue & worker —
work that runs in the background instead of making the user wait, held in a waiting line (queue) and picked up by a separate process (worker).
Application Challenge

Design (schema + validation rule, not necessarily working code) a guardrail for a tool this course hasn't built — argue for the one field you'd validate first and what specifically could go wrong if you skipped it.

Knowledge check
  • Why can't you trust every model response without checking it first?
  • Why move long-running work to a background job instead of making the user wait?

Week 4 — HOS 4: Mobile Client, Push & Observability

Merges legacy Weeks 8–9.

A real mobile client wired to the backend with push notifications, instrumented with tracing so its behavior is observable in production — full-stack, end to end.

Outcome 4 Outcome 5 Open hands-on guide →
New words this week
Push notification & push token —
a message sent to a device even when the app isn't open, addressed using a unique ID (push token) for that device.
Observability & trace —
being able to see what a running system is actually doing (observability), via a recorded record of one request's full journey (trace).
Application Challenge

Propose an observability plan (what to trace, what to score, what alert you'd want) for a different kind of AI feature than this course built — argue for what would actually be worth watching versus what would just be noise.

Knowledge check
  • What has to happen before your phone can receive a push notification?
  • If a user reports a wrong answer, what would you check first to figure out why?

Week 5 — Checkpoint: Architecture & Bottleneck Review

No new build. Students diagram the combined architecture across all 4 HOS and identify where nondeterminism or latency could break the system — now reviewing a complete picture instead of one week's slice of it.

Outcome 3
Review prompt — no new vocabulary this week
  • Can you draw, from memory, the path a message takes across all 4 HOS, start to finish?
  • Where in that path could something slow down or fail?

Week 6 — Integration & Alternate Review

A deliberate pause: no new build, no new tech. Concept debate connecting all 4 HOS into one coherent system, and time to scope and form project teams (or confirm going individual) before Team/Term Project work begins.

Review prompt
  • Which of the 4 HOS depends on which other one, and in what order do they actually run?
  • What's one thing you'd want to double-check before starting the term project?

Weeks 7–9 — Team/Term Project

Students (team or individual, their choice) analyze and apply the 4 HOS together — the project is explicitly a synthesis of what was built in Weeks 1–4, not a new concept. Three weeks of work time, with instructor check-ins. Use the Capstone Prompt Builder to help scope and structure what you're asking an AI assistant to scaffold for your specific project.

Progress checks
  • Week 7: Scope locked — which HOS pieces your project combines and why, in writing, plus a rough architecture sketch. Roles assigned if it's a team.
  • Week 8: A rough, working version exists — something a partner or the instructor could actually run, touching at least two of the combined HOS pieces end to end.
  • Week 9: Feature-complete and rehearsed — everything works together, remaining time goes to polish and practicing the Week 10 demo, not new features.

Week 10 — Final Presentation & Demo

Live demonstration of the Team/Term Project, touching every outcome the course covered.

Self-check before you present
  • Can you explain, without notes, how each of the 4 HOS shows up in your final project?
