Course Description

Agentic AI empowers students to design intelligent systems capable of perceiving their environment, reasoning through complex, multi-step plans, utilizing external tools (such as APIs, web browsers, and databases), and self-correcting their actions to achieve specific outcomes without ongoing human intervention. This course emphasizes the core principles of agency, including individual agencies, the development of custom tools, advanced reasoning techniques, multi-agent workflows, and orchestration. By the end of the course, students will have built professional portfolios featuring a range of functional agent examples, acquired industry-ready AI engineering skills, and developed agentic mindsets to transform manual business processes into automated sequences of agent-driven tasks.

Course Outcomes

This course will prepare students to:

CO1 Explain the core components of an "AI Agent" (Reasoning, Planning, Tool Use, and Memory), distinguish them from standard LLM inference, and identify common agentic patterns.

CO2 Implement custom tools and integrate them into an agentic loop via function calling.

CO3 Analyze agent reasoning traces to diagnose why an agent failed at a given task, such as identifying tool-call errors or logic loops.

CO4 Assess agent system performance through agent-on-agent testing and quantitative metrics, including success rate, cost per task, and latency.

CO5 Produce a professional-grade portfolio project that automates a multi-step business process, such as an autonomous marketing department or a self-healing coding assistant.

AI Challenge (AIC) (40% of Final Grade) CO2, CO3 & CO4

The AI Challenge (AIC) is designed to support “learning-by-example” and “learning-by-doing.” Students learn to
create a system by reorganizing elements into a new pattern or structure through system
development or virtual labs.

Each AIC runs over two weeks: Part A: Build in the first week and Part B: Apply in the second. It is graded once, from the last push to its repository before the end of its second week, with one rubric of nine criteria worth 100 points: five Build criteria for Part A (12% each) and four Challenge criteria for Part B (Application 4%, Analysis 8%, Evaluation 12% and Design 16%).

In Part B, students apply what they learned to a smaller, individual task in a new scenario and make an open design decision. The challenge is completed individually in the challenge/ folder of the same AIC repository.

Part B ends with a short Post Mortem in NOTES.md, written without AI: the idea from the module that is still least clear, one thing the student would do differently next time (including how they used AI), and one thing they will carry into the Term Project. It is graded under Understand; a missing or one-line Post Mortem cannot earn full Understand marks.

Term Project (40% of Final Grade) CO1, CO3, CO4 & CO5

Students design, build, and present one multi-agent system that automates a realistic multi-step business process, such as an autonomous marketing department or a self-healing coding assistant, working on their own or in a group of up to three (four with instructor approval). The system combines all four AICs:

  • An agentic loop with reasoning, planning, and tool calling (AIC 1)

  • Custom tools served over MCP, with short- and long-term memory (AIC 2)

  • Reasoning traces and self-correction (AIC 3)

  • Multi-agent orchestration and agent-on-agent evaluation (AIC 4)

It also applies to the judgment practiced in the AIC challenges, such as which actions the model may take on its own and when the system should be handed off to a person.

The Term Project is 40% of the final grade: 200 points for the team deliverables below and 200 points for an individual Interview in Week 9 or Week 10 (see TP Interview below). Half of each student’s Term Project grade is therefore earned individually.

Each Term Project deliverable is mapped to one course outcome, shown in its rubric heading and in the Course Outcome Alignment table.

TP03 Demo Video (1-2 Minutes, 10 points) CO5

Requiring a short YouTube demo serves as proof of functional execution and concise technical communication:

  • Proof of Functional Execution: In agent systems built on LLMs, code can appear complete on paper yet fail in practice due to issues like API versioning, dependency mismatches, a tool server that won’t start, or invalid API credentials. A live demo video proves the agents run end-to-end beyond "Code on GitHub."

  • Concise Technical Communication: The 1–2-minute limit requires students to avoid rambling and distill their project to its core: the user problem, the agents’ steps and tool calls, and the result. This format mirrors real-world tech pitch demos and pulls request video walkthroughs.

To prevent common submission issues, share these instructions with your teams:

TP Interview (individual, 200 points) CO1

In Week 9 or Week 10, each student has a 15-minute, one-on-one interview with the instructor about their Term Project, run like a technical job interview rather than a presentation. There are no slides: the instructor leads with questions, and the student answers with the running system, the repository and the traces open. Students in a team are interviewed and scored individually, on their own answers.

The interview has six stages: a 60-second pitch, a deep dive tracing one request through the system, design trade-offs, a debugging scenario, ownership and AI use, and the student’s own questions. Sign-up slots are posted at the start of Week 9. AI assistants may not be used during the interview. Students with approved accommodations may request extra time or another format.

A missing interview earns 0 points. Each criterion is scored at its level’s midpoint (96%, 83%, 68% or 30%) unless the notes clearly put it higher or lower in the band.

Concept Debate (10% of Final Grade) CO1, CO3

Students engage in a structured discussion of their reasoning with classmates. Each week comes with a Concept Debate question, already provided in the course, rooted in that week’s key concepts: Debate A in the first week of each AIC, on the module’s core idea (CO1); Debate B in the second week, on a design trade-off (CO3); and integration debates in Weeks 9–10 (CO3). Each question has two defensible sides, and students consider both before taking a position.

Before the first debate, each student joins one of two groups. The instructor may assign students, or they may choose a group, as long as the groups remain roughly equal in size. The groups alternate between arguing Side A and Side B in successive debates.

Each week, students complete a three-stage response sequence:

  1. Initial position (assigned side): Present a clear argument for the side your debate group is assigned, using a specific claim and supporting evidence rather than a general summary.

  2. Argue the opposite side: Before responding to a classmate, post the strongest argument for the opposing side, the position you did not initially hold. Because everyone engages with both perspectives, “I agree with you” is not a valid response.

  3. Synthesis: Set aside advocacy and state a genuine synthesis or consensus position. Reference at least one specific point from a classmate’s post, and avoid generic responses like “great points, I agree.”

Knowledge Check (10% of Final Grade) CO1, CO3

Weekly quizzes assess your understanding of key concepts learned through the AICs. Emphasize grasping underlying principles over rote memorization when preparing for the quizzes. Each question has a 90-second time limit, simulating the pace of industry certification exams.

There are ten Knowledge Checks, one per week, worth 10 points each. In each AIC, the first-week check covers theory: the concepts from the lecture and readings (CO1). The second-week check covers skills: reading code, logs, and outputs like those in the build (CO3). The Week 9 and Week 10 checks are cumulative.

Students are required to adhere to the honor code, which strictly prohibits the use of AI assistants. You may retake each Knowledge Check as many times as needed, and your highest score counts. Each question is worth 2 points, for 10 points per check, and is scored automatically. After every attempt, you will receive feedback on any questions answered incorrectly to help guide your learning.
