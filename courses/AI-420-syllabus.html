Course Description

Agentic AI empowers students to design intelligent systems capable of perceiving their environment, reasoning through complex, multi-step plans, utilizing external tools (such as APIs, web browsers, and databases), and self-correcting their actions to achieve specific outcomes without ongoing human intervention. This course emphasizes the core principles of agency, including individual agencies, the development of custom tools, advanced reasoning techniques, multi-agent workflows, and orchestration. By the end of the course, students will have built professional portfolios featuring a range of functional agent examples, acquired industry-ready AI engineering skills, and developed agentic mindsets to transform manual business processes into automated sequences of agent-driven tasks.

Course Outcomes

As a result of this course, students will know or be able to do the following:

  1. O1 — Explain the core components of an "AI Agent" (Reasoning, Planning, Tool Use, and Memory), distinguish them from standard LLM inference, and identify common agentic patterns.
  2. O2 — Implement custom tools and integrate them into an agentic loop via function calling.
  3. O3 — Analyze agent reasoning traces to diagnose why an agent failed at a given task, such as identifying tool-call errors or logic loops.
  4. O4 — Assess agent system performance through agent-on-agent testing and quantitative metrics, including success rate, cost per task, and latency.
  5. O5 — Produce a professional-grade portfolio project that automates a multi-step business process, such as an autonomous marketing department or a self-healing coding assistant.

Core Concepts & Tools

  • The four agent components — reasoning, planning, tool use, memory — and how an agent differs from a single model call; a ReAct loop written by hand, then the same agent in Google ADK
  • Agentic patterns: ReAct, plan-and-execute, reflection, and routing
  • Custom tools for APIs, web browsers, and databases, with function-calling schemas, served over the Model Context Protocol (MCP)
  • Agent memory: short-term session state and long-term memory in a local vector store (Chroma)
  • Reasoning traces and failure diagnosis (local traces and Langfuse): tool-call errors, invented actions, logic loops, confident wrong answers
  • Self-correction: tool errors returned as observations, repeated-call detection, and step budgets, attached as ADK callbacks
  • Multi-agent orchestration: a coordinator delegating to specialist agents
  • Agent-on-agent evaluation: a simulated user, an LLM judge checked against human grading, and success rate, cost per task, and p50/p95 latency
  • A professional portfolio repo in GitHub Codespaces, with one committed agent per HOS, on the free Gemini API tier

Suggested MP format — a structured, standing submission (not a blank box)

The most common failure mode for an async Muddiest Point is students posting a low-effort, generic line ("this week was confusing") just to get participation credit. Rather than one form due at the end of the week, adopt the model used by ACQUIRED (2025) — a standing, always-open submission point available as soon as that week's material is posted, due before the HOS build begins — and require four short fields instead of a free-text box:

  1. What you were doing right before you got confused (the specific concept, video timestamp, or line of the guide).
  2. The exact place you got stuck — not "I don't get agents," but "I don't understand why the loop appends the model's own function-call turn to the history before the tool result."
  3. What you already tried — re-reading, a specific search, asking a classmate — before submitting.
  4. Optional: your best guess at the answer, even if you think it's wrong.

Requiring context + a specific point + a prior attempt makes a one-line non-answer structurally harder to submit than an actual muddiest point — and gives the instructor something concrete to respond to. ACQUIRED's own student feedback ranked "ease of asking questions" and the modular, organized format above the MP prompt itself, suggesting structure — not stricter policing — is what improves submission quality.

Suggested CD format — argue both sides, then synthesize (not "pick a side and defend it")

A freeform "state your position, respond to a peer" debate tends toward one of two failure modes: there's an obviously-correct answer, so responses collapse into "I agree" with no real engagement, or the debate has no real stakes so nobody pushes back. Mitchell (2019) found that assigning a position (not letting students choose) drove real engagement precisely because students had to argue something they didn't personally hold — and Structured Academic Controversy adds a further structural fix directly aimed at the "devolves into I Agree" problem: require students to argue both sides before any consensus is allowed. A 3-stage sequence within the week:

  1. Initial position (assigned side): argue the side your debate team was assigned for this HOS (see below), with a specific claim and evidence — not a general summary of the topic.
  2. Argue the opposite side: before responding to any classmate, students must post the strongest version of the other side's argument — the one they didn't hold. This is the step that prevents "I agree with you" from being a viable response, since everyone has now argued both sides.
  3. Synthesis: drop advocacy entirely and state a genuine synthesis or consensus position, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post (not just "great points, I agree").

Grading rewards engagement with a specific argument someone else made — a response that only affirms agreement without citing what it's agreeing with doesn't meet the Writing criterion above.

How CD sides are assigned: Brightspace debate teams, rotating each HOS

Sides are assigned automatically, so faculty never assign them by hand. In Week 1, Brightspace randomly and evenly splits the class into Debate Team 1 and Debate Team 2 (instructor setup: Communication → Groups → New Category, with "# of Groups – Auto Enrollment", 2 groups, and "Randomize users in groups" and "Auto-enroll new users" checked). Students keep their team all term, and the sides rotate so nobody argues the same kind of position every time:

Team/Term Project — Description

The Team/Term Project automates one real, multi-step business process end to end with a team of agents, and reports how well they do it — the course description's "agentic mindset" in practice. Every technical piece you need, you built once in HOS 1–4; the work here is synthesis: map a manual process, decide which steps are agents, deterministic code, or justified human checkpoints, then build, evaluate, and present it.

Worth 20% of your final grade. Team or individual, your choice — teams of 2–4 are typical. Introduced in Week 1 (nothing due yet), a lightweight checkpoint at the end of Week 6, an architecture review at the end of Week 8, then a compact plan-then-build Week 9 and a finish-and-demo Week 10. It becomes the centerpiece of your portfolio.

Example project — an autonomous marketing department for a small business

  • HOS 1 (agent loop and patterns): a plan-and-execute coordinator that turns "promote next month's workshop" into research, draft, review, and schedule steps.
  • HOS 2 (custom tools, MCP, memory): tools to read the business's website, look up past campaign results in a database, and save brand-voice rules to long-term memory.
  • HOS 3 (traces, self-correction): a brand-review step that rejects off-voice drafts and sends them back, and a repeat-call guard, both visible in Langfuse traces.
  • HOS 4 (multi-agent, evaluation): researcher, copywriter, and reviewer agents, tested on 15+ scenarios by a simulated business owner and a judge, with success rate, cost per campaign, and latency.

A self-healing coding assistant (read failing test → diagnose → patch → re-run → open PR) is the course description's other example. Litmus test for any idea: can you point to one part and say "this is HOS 3, and here's the trace showing it recover"?

Minimum bar

  • Runs end to end from a single trigger, with no human clicks between steps except justified checkpoints.
  • At least 3 custom tools (served over MCP), and at least 2 cooperating agents.
  • At least one self-correction mechanism, shown working in a real trace.
  • An eval suite of 15+ agent-on-agent scenarios, reporting success rate, cost per task, and latency.
  • A portfolio README a hiring manager could read in 3 minutes: the problem, architecture diagram, metrics, one failure and how it was fixed, and a demo video link.

Team/Term Project — Collaboration, Planning & Execution

With all four HOS weeks claimed through Week 8, planning happens fast, in the first few days of Week 9, using a lightweight version of Scrum. That's only possible because you've had the project idea in mind since Week 1 and gave it a written check-in at the end of Week 6 — Week 9 isn't starting cold. Start with a process map: shadow or interview someone who does the process, list its steps, and decide for each one whether it's an agent, deterministic code, or a human checkpoint — and why.

Scrum-lite, in four pieces

  1. Product Backlog — a plain list of everything the project might need, written as user stories: "As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]." It doesn't need to be complete or ordered yet — just get every idea out of your head and onto the list. Do this in the first day or two of Week 9.
  2. Sprint Planning — pick a small, realistic subset of the backlog you can actually finish in the build time left (the rest of Week 9 plus Week 10). This becomes your Sprint 1 plan. Anything left over stays backlog, not a promise.
  3. Standups — brief, regular check-ins once building starts (async in a group chat is fine): what you did, what's next, what's blocking you. For a team, this is what catches "I've been stuck for three days" before demo week instead of during it. Solo students can keep this as a personal daily log.
  4. Sprint Review — the Week 10 demo itself is your sprint review: show what actually works, and be honest about what didn't make it in.
