Course Description

Agentic AI empowers students to design intelligent systems capable of perceiving their environment, reasoning through complex, multi-step plans, utilizing external tools (such as APIs, web browsers, and databases), and self-correcting their actions to achieve specific outcomes without ongoing human intervention. This course emphasizes the core principles of agency, including individual agencies, the development of custom tools, advanced reasoning techniques, multi-agent workflows, and orchestration. By the end of the course, students will have built professional portfolios featuring a range of functional agent examples, acquired industry-ready AI engineering skills, and developed agentic mindsets to transform manual business processes into automated sequences of agent-driven tasks.

Course Outcomes

This course will prepare students to:

CO1 Explain the core components of an "AI Agent" (Reasoning, Planning, Tool Use, and Memory), distinguish them from standard LLM inference, and identify common agentic patterns.

CO2 Implement custom tools and integrate them into an agentic loop via function calling.

CO3 Analyze agent reasoning traces to diagnose why an agent failed at a given task, such as identifying tool-call errors or logic loops.

CO4 Assess agent system performance through agent-on-agent testing and quantitative metrics, including success rate, cost per task, and latency.

CO5 Produce a professional-grade portfolio project that automates a multi-step business process, such as an autonomous marketing department or a self-healing coding assistant.

Additional Information

Core Concepts & Tools

  • The four agent components (reasoning, planning, tool use, and memory) and how an agent differs from a single LLM inference call

  • Agent-first development with Google Antigravity (directing a coding agent through plans, task lists, and reviewable artifacts), then agents built in Google ADK on the Gemini API: function tools, the run loop, events, and the adk web developer UI

  • Agentic patterns in ADK: tool-calling loops (ReAct), plan-and-execute, reflection loops, and routing with workflow graphs

  • Custom tools for APIs, web pages, and databases: typed function tools with errors returned as data, and tools served over the Model Context Protocol (MCP) and connected with McpToolset

  • Agent memory: short-term session state, long-term user state that survives a restart, and ADK memory services searched by the agent

  • Reasoning traces and failure diagnosis from ADK events and traces: tool-call errors, invented actions, logic loops, and confident wrong answers

  • Self-correction without human intervention: errors returned to the model, callbacks that stop repeated calls and enforce step budgets, retries, and reflection loops, with human confirmation for risky actions

  • Multi-agent orchestration and agent-on-agent evaluation: a coordinator with sub-agents and workflow graphs, ADK evaluation (adk eval) with a simulated user and an LLM judge checked against human grading, and success rate, cost per task, and latency

  • AC 1 — Anatomy of an Agent

Students build the loop for a fictional travel agency’s trip-planning agent (search flights, check hotel availability, convert currency), including its step budget and stopping rules, and must pass the provided tests. The open decision is what the agent tells a customer when it runs out of steps before the trip is fully planned. Students also decide which of five requests from the agency need an agent at all, and which a single model call or a fixed script would handle better.

  • AC 2 — Custom Tools, MCP, & Memory
    Students rebuild a badly written tool set for a fictional property manager’s maintenance agent (look up a unit, open a work order, read a vendor’s web page) as an MCP server with typed schemas and errors returned as data, and must pass the provided tests. The open decision is which tenant details the agent may keep in long-term memory and which it must never store. Students also predict how a model would have misused each original tool.

  • AC 3 — Traces & Self-Correction
    Students receive recorded traces from a fictional food bank’s volunteer-scheduling agent, without its source code, and name each failure and the step where it first appears. They then build guards that make the recorded failing runs pass the provided replay tests. The open decision is when the agent should stop retrying and hand the request to a staff member. Students also explain which failures their guards cannot catch.

  • AC 4 — Multi-Agent Orchestration & Evaluation
    Students build the evaluation for a vendor’s invoice-processing agent team, which claims a 94% success rate: a judge, a check of the judge against human grades, and success rate, cost per invoice, and latency. They run a recorded set of conversations through it. The open decision is the success rate and cost per invoice at which the company should adopt the system. Students also explain what the vendor’s claim left out.

Project Description
Students design, build, and present one multi-agent system that automates a realistic multi-step business process, such as an autonomous marketing department or a self-healing coding assistant, working on their own or in a group of up to three (four with instructor approval). The system combines all four HOS:

  • An agentic loop with reasoning, planning, and tool calling (HOS 1)

  • Custom tools served over MCP, with short- and long-term memory (HOS 2)

  • Reasoning traces and self-correction (HOS 3)

  • Multi-agent orchestration and agent-on-agent evaluation (HOS 4)

It also applies to the judgment practiced in the Application Challenges, such as which actions the model may take on its own and when the system should be handed off to a person.

Working Solo or in a Group

You may complete the Term Project on your own or in a group of up to three students; a group of four needs the instructor’s approval. Decide by the end of Week 1. Solo and group projects have the same deliverables, points, and scope (all four HOS). Wherever this section refers to a “team,” a solo student is a team of one; criteria about splitting work among members are graded on the evidence one person can show, such as steady commits across the term and use of feedback. A group’s roles and responsibilities must be clearly described in its final report.

Report
The student will provide a report formatted based on a template provided by the instructor. Students are required to improve their writing iteratively and incrementally every week. The revision will always happen during a quarter. Students will add new required sections to the existing paper every week.    

The final report is the culmination of applied research and activities conducted throughout the quarter. The final report provides a detailed problem, and its solution is likely to be encountered by a company or organization described in a case study supplied by the student. 

The paper is to be between 5 and 6 pages. The required template for class submissions comes from an international organization, ISCAP (Information Systems & Computing Academic Professionals). The instructor may recommend teams submit their paper to conferences. Submissions are optional and will not impact the course grade. Additional revisions may be required after the course.    

The file name consists of the project phase, report type, team number, and the list of your team members. Each report must be maintained in OneDrive to trace team collaboration. Each report must include the team’s GitHub repository named after the keywords. Also, the final report must include the YouTube link for the demo. Three report submissions are required according to the following schedule:  

  • Proposal (1 page; 10 points)

    • Duration: 3 weeks (Week 1 – Week 3) 

    • Purpose: Start with preliminary case studies and focus heavily on the problem definition, motivation, background, key related work, and proposed approach, and ISCAP template compliance.

    • Report Name in OneDrive: “AI420 TP T0X Proposal Author1 Author2 Author3.docx”

As in any scholarly writing, students should not merely copy information from another author. Students should use evidence to support the contentions they have drawn from their findings and critically analyze related literature. In essence, each paper needs to be an analytical paper, not a summary of readings.  

Students are expected to use the assigned readings, videos, and other materials throughout the quarter. Students will need to utilize additional sources that were not assigned by the professor. While stylized after an industry report, students are expected to employ APA formatting for citations, footnotes, and bibliography. Students must cite the sources of all ideas, facts, and information used that are not their own, even if they have put the information into their own words. Failure to do so is plagiarism, although the oversight is unintentional. To avoid plagiarism, check https://library.cityu.edu/howto/apa-writing/avoid-plagiarism/.

The Muddiest Point (MP) - Grading Criteria

Before starting each module, students are required to submit the Muddiest Point (MP) activity. The purpose of this activity is to stimulate student engagement. The instructor uses the MP to assess how students understood each module’s HOS and its required readings. The instructor also uses the MP to customize the lecture scope to implement Just-in-Time Teaching (JiTT).

The MP consists of four short fields:

  1. What you were doing right before you got confused (the specific concept, video timestamp, or line of the guide).

  2. The exact place you got stuck — not "I don't get agents," but "I don't understand why ADK sends the tool's result back to the model before the agent answers."

  3. What you already tried — re-reading, a specific search, asking a classmate — before submitting.

  4. Optional: your best guess at the answer, even if you think it's wrong.

Concept Debate (CD) – Grading Criteria

Students engage in a structured discussion of their reasoning with classmates. Each HOS comes with a Concept Debate question, already provided in the course, rooted in HOS’s key concepts. Each question has two defensible sides, and students consider both before taking a position.

Before the first debate, each student joins one of two groups. The instructor may assign students, or they may choose a group, as long as the groups remain roughly equal in size. The groups alternate between arguing Side A and Side B in successive debates.

In the first week of each HOS, students complete a three-stage response sequence:

  1. Initial position (assigned side): Present a clear argument for the side your debate group is assigned, using a specific claim and supporting evidence rather than a general summary.

  2. Argue the opposite side: Before responding to a classmate, post the strongest argument for the opposing side, the position you did not initially hold. Because everyone engages with both perspectives, “I agree with you” is not a valid response.

  3. Synthesis: Set aside advocacy and state a genuine synthesis or consensus position. Reference at least one specific point from a classmate’s post, and avoid generic responses like “great points, I agree.”

Knowledge Check (KC)
Weekly quizzes assess your understanding of key concepts learned through HOSs. Emphasize grasping underlying principles over rote memorization when preparing for the quizzes. Each question has a 90-second time limit, simulating the pace of industry certification exams.

Students are required to adhere to the honor code, which strictly prohibits the use of AI assistants. You may retake each exam as many times as needed, and a passing score is set at 80%. After every attempt, you will receive feedback on any questions answered incorrectly to help guide your learning.

Non-Discrimination & Prohibition of Sexual Harassment

City University of Seattle adheres to all federal, state, and local civil rights laws prohibiting discrimination in employment and education. The University is committed to ensuring that the educational environment is bound by standards of mutual respect and safety and is free from discriminatory practices.

In the U.S., the University is required by Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972 to ensure that all its education programs and activities do not discriminate based on sex/gender. Sex includes sex, sex stereotypes, gender identity, gender expression, sexual orientation, and pregnancy or parenting status. Sexual harassment, sexual assault, dating and domestic violence, and stalking are forms of sex discrimination, which are prohibited under Title IX and by City University of Seattle policy. City University of Seattle also prohibits retaliation against any person opposing discrimination or participating in any discrimination investigation or complaint process internal or external to the institution. Questions regarding Title IX, including its application and/or concerns about noncompliance, should be directed to the Title IX Coordinator. For a complete copy of the policy or for more information, visit the CityU website or contact the Title IX Coordinator.

In Canada, in compliance with the British Columbia Human Rights Code, the Alberta Human Rights Act, WorksafeBC, and the Workers’ Compensation Board of Alberta, the University believes that its environment should always be supportive and respectful of the dignity and self-esteem of individuals. Discrimination, harassment, and bullying conduct, whether through person-to-person behavior or via electronic communications such as email or social media, is not acceptable and will not be tolerated. As an educational institution, we are responsible for cultivating an environment of excellence, equity, and mutual respect, and for recognizing the value and potential of every individual. The University will take all necessary steps to meet or exceed the requirements of the law to prevent discrimination, harassment, and bullying. The Respectful Workplace Policy for the prevention of discrimination, harassment, and bullying is available on the CityU in Canada website.
