Course Description

Explores the convergence of AI and the physical world through Digital Twins and Physical AI. Topics include high-fidelity 3D modeling, IoT real-time data assimilation, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), synthetic data generation (SDG), sim-to-real robotics, and ethics of autonomous systems in safety-critical domains.

Course Outcomes

As a result of this course, students will know or be able to do the following:

  1. O1 — Explain “Digital Shadows” vs. “Digital Twins” and design key architectural components: physical entity, virtual entity, and data connection.
  2. O2 — Apply Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to ensure AI predictions in virtual environments align with real-world physical laws.
  3. O3 — Develop real-time data pipelines that synchronize sensor data with virtual models.
  4. O4 — Construct photorealistic, physically accurate simulation environments to generate synthetic data for training physical AI models.
  5. O5 — Conduct “what-if” scenario testing within digital twins to predict system failures and assess safety of autonomous actions before deployment.
  6. O6 — Design and deploy a functional digital twin for a targeted use case (smart building, drone, or manufacturing cell) demonstrating real-time monitoring and autonomous decision-making.

Core Concepts & Tools

Tools are proposals pending the feasibility test. Real hardware is out of scope; sensors are simulated, and everything must run on CPU in a Codespace.

  • Digital models vs. digital shadows vs. digital twins; the three components: physical entity, virtual entity, data connection
  • Real-time IoT pipelines: MQTT publish/subscribe, flows, time-series storage, live dashboards (proposed: Mosquitto, Node-RED, InfluxDB, Grafana in Docker Compose; a Python sensor simulator or Wokwi)
  • End-to-end latency, data loss, and sensor faults (stuck values, spikes, dropouts) and how to detect them
  • Physics-informed neural networks: the data term, the physics residual, boundary and initial conditions, and the balance between them (proposed: PyTorch on CPU, then DeepXDE)
  • Physically accurate simulation and high-fidelity 3D scenes (proposed: MuJoCo, OpenUSD, BlenderProc)
  • Synthetic data generation with perfect labels; domain randomization; the sim-to-real gap measured against altered physics (proposed: Gymnasium + stable-baselines3 on CPU)
  • What-if scenario testing as parameter sweeps (proposed: SimPy or MuJoCo), and safety envelopes for autonomous actions
  • An autonomous agent that reads the twin and acts over MQTT inside a safety envelope (proposed: Google ADK), with every action logged
  • Ethics of autonomous systems in safety-critical domains: accountability, human oversight, and what a twin must never decide alone

Suggested MP format — a structured, standing submission (not a blank box)

The most common failure mode for an async Muddiest Point is students posting a low-effort, generic line ("this week was confusing") just to get participation credit. Rather than one form due at the end of the week, adopt the model used by ACQUIRED (2025) — a standing, always-open submission point available as soon as that week's material is posted, due before the HOS build begins — and require four short fields instead of a free-text box:

  1. What you were doing right before you got confused (the specific concept, video timestamp, or line of the guide).
  2. The exact place you got stuck — not "I don't get digital twins," but "I don't understand whether a twin that only recommends a setpoint, which a person then applies, still counts as a twin."
  3. What you already tried — re-reading, a specific search, asking a classmate — before submitting.
  4. Optional: your best guess at the answer, even if you think it's wrong.

Requiring context + a specific point + a prior attempt makes a one-line non-answer structurally harder to submit than an actual muddiest point — and gives the instructor something concrete to respond to. ACQUIRED's own student feedback ranked "ease of asking questions" and the modular, organized format above the MP prompt itself, suggesting structure — not stricter policing — is what improves submission quality.

Suggested CD format — argue both sides, then synthesize (not "pick a side and defend it")

A freeform "state your position, respond to a peer" debate tends toward one of two failure modes: there's an obviously-correct answer, so responses collapse into "I agree" with no real engagement, or the debate has no real stakes so nobody pushes back. Mitchell (2019) found that assigning a position (not letting students choose) drove real engagement precisely because students had to argue something they didn't personally hold — and Structured Academic Controversy adds a further structural fix directly aimed at the "devolves into I Agree" problem: require students to argue both sides before any consensus is allowed. A 3-stage sequence within the week:

  1. Initial position (assigned side): argue the side your debate team was assigned for this HOS (see below), with a specific claim and evidence — not a general summary of the topic.
  2. Argue the opposite side: before responding to any classmate, students must post the strongest version of the other side's argument — the one they didn't hold. This is the step that prevents "I agree with you" from being a viable response, since everyone has now argued both sides.
  3. Synthesis: drop advocacy entirely and state a genuine synthesis or consensus position, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post (not just "great points, I agree").

Grading rewards engagement with a specific argument someone else made — a response that only affirms agreement without citing what it's agreeing with doesn't meet the Writing criterion above.

How CD sides are assigned: Brightspace debate teams, rotating each HOS

Sides are assigned automatically, so faculty never assign them by hand. In Week 1, Brightspace randomly and evenly splits the class into Debate Team 1 and Debate Team 2 (instructor setup: Communication → Groups → New Category, with "# of Groups – Auto Enrollment", 2 groups, and "Randomize users in groups" and "Auto-enroll new users" checked). Students keep their team all term, and the sides rotate so nobody argues the same kind of position every time:

Each CD is still one whole-class discussion topic, not a separate area per group, because Stages 2 and 3 require reading the other team's posts. Students find their team under Communication → Groups.

No team? (for example, a late add) Before posting Stage 1, count the posts on each side and join the side with fewer; if they're tied, take Side A. Team/Term Project — Description

The Team/Term Project designs and deploys the functional digital twin in Outcome O6 for one of three use cases — a smart building, a drone, or a manufacturing cell — with real-time monitoring and autonomous decision-making. Sensors are simulated; everything else is real: live data driving the virtual model, physics-aware predictions, what-if testing, and autonomous actions that stay inside a proven safety envelope.

Worth 20% of your final grade. Team or individual, your choice. Introduced in Week 1, a checkpoint at the end of Week 6, a three-component architecture review at the end of Week 8, then a compact plan-then-build Week 9 and a finish-and-demo Week 10.

Example project — one floor of a smart building

  • HOS 1: temperature and occupancy sensors simulated and streaming live into the virtual floor.
  • HOS 2: a PINN that predicts how heat spreads through the floor and respects the heat equation.
  • HOS 3–4: what-if runs (an HVAC unit fails on the hottest day) to find where temperatures become unsafe.
  • Decisions: an agent that adjusts setpoints on its own, but only inside a safety envelope the what-if tests proved, with every action logged and monitored live.

Minimum bar

  • Live sensor data (simulated is fine) visibly driving the virtual model in real time.
  • At least one prediction constrained by physics (a PINN or a physics check), with evidence it helps.
  • At least one what-if scenario that changed a design or safety decision.
  • An autonomous action taken, logged, and blocked when it would have been unsafe, plus a short ethics note on who is accountable if the twin is wrong.

Team/Term Project — Collaboration, Planning & Execution

With all four HOS weeks claimed through Week 8, planning happens fast, in the first few days of Week 9, using a lightweight version of Scrum. That's only possible because you've had the project idea in mind since Week 1 and gave it a written check-in at the end of Week 6 — Week 9 isn't starting cold.

Scrum-lite, in four pieces

  1. Product Backlog — a plain list of everything the project might need, written as user stories: "As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]." It doesn't need to be complete or ordered yet — just get every idea out of your head and onto the list. Do this in the first day or two of Week 9.
  2. Sprint Planning — pick a small, realistic subset of the backlog you can actually finish in the build time left (the rest of Week 9 plus Week 10). This becomes your Sprint 1 plan. Anything left over stays backlog, not a promise.
  3. Standups — brief, regular check-ins once building starts (async in a group chat is fine): what you did, what's next, what's blocking you. For a team, this is what catches "I've been stuck for three days" before demo week instead of during it. Solo students can keep this as a personal daily log.
  4. Sprint Review — the Week 10 demo itself is your sprint review: show what actually works, and be honest about what didn't make it in.
