In plain terms: a digital twin is a live virtual copy of something physical, such as a building, a drone, or a factory cell, kept in sync by sensor data. This course builds one: stream the sensor data in, make its predictions obey physics, test dangerous "what-ifs" in simulation instead of in real life, and let an AI make decisions only once they've been proven safe there.

Course Description: Explores the convergence of AI and the physical world through Digital Twins and Physical AI. Topics include high-fidelity 3D modeling, IoT real-time data assimilation, physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), synthetic data generation (SDG), sim-to-real robotics, and ethics of autonomous systems in safety-critical domains.

Course Outcomes

As a result of this course, students will know or be able to do the following:

  1. O1 — Explain “Digital Shadows” vs. “Digital Twins” and design key architectural components: physical entity, virtual entity, and data connection.
  2. O2 — Apply Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to ensure AI predictions in virtual environments align with real-world physical laws.
  3. O3 — Develop real-time data pipelines that synchronize sensor data with virtual models.
  4. O4 — Construct photorealistic, physically accurate simulation environments to generate synthetic data for training physical AI models.
  5. O5 — Conduct “what-if” scenario testing within digital twins to predict system failures and assess safety of autonomous actions before deployment.
  6. O6 — Design and deploy a functional digital twin for a targeted use case (smart building, drone, or manufacturing cell) demonstrating real-time monitoring and autonomous decision-making.

Week 8 architecture review (folded in): draw the twin's three parts, physical entity, virtual entity, and data connection, for the system built across HOS 1–4, and say exactly what would have to change to turn a digital shadow into a twin. It's the direct check on O1.

Team/Term Project

Design and deploy the functional digital twin in O6, for one of the three named use cases: a smart building, a drone, or a manufacturing cell. It needs real-time monitoring and autonomous decision-making. Team or individual.

Example project
  • Use case: one floor of a smart building, with temperature and occupancy sensors simulated (HOS 1).
  • HOS 2: a PINN that predicts how heat spreads through the floor and respects the heat equation.
  • HOS 3–4: what-if runs (an HVAC unit fails on the hottest day) to find where temperatures become unsafe.
  • Decisions: an agent that adjusts setpoints on its own, but only inside a safety envelope that the what-if tests proved, with every action logged. Monitored live in Grafana.
Minimum bar
  • Live sensor data (simulated is fine) visibly driving the virtual model in real time.
  • At least one prediction constrained by physics (a PINN or a physics check), with evidence it helps.
  • At least one what-if scenario that changed a design or safety decision.
  • An autonomous action taken, logged, and blocked when it would have been unsafe, plus a short ethics note on who is accountable if the twin is wrong.

Planning works the same way as in AI 410 and AI 420: early in Week 9, a few days of Scrum-lite planning (a short backlog of user stories, roles if it's a team, a free PM tool; GitHub Projects recommended, Trello or Notion also work), then straight into building, with standups while you build and the Week 10 demo as the review. You've had the idea since Week 1 and a checkpoint in Week 6, so Week 9 doesn't start cold.

Feasibility Checklist — Before Any Content Is Built

What has to be tried in a real Codespace (2-core first, then 4-core if needed) on free tiers before a unit's guide is written. If a tool fails, swap it for another that meets the same outcome. Never change the outcome.

HOSWhat to test
HOS 1All four Docker services running together on a 2-core Codespace, with Node-RED and Grafana UIs through forwarded ports. Whether Wokwi in the browser can reach an MQTT broker in the Codespace, or needs a free public broker (e.g. HiveMQ).
HOS 2Training time for a small PINN (e.g. heat flow or a damped spring) on 2 CPU cores, for both plain PyTorch and DeepXDE. The target is minutes, not hours.
HOS 3The highest-risk unit, because O4 says "photorealistic" and there's no GPU. Headless MuJoCo rendering (EGL/OSMesa) in a Codespace. BlenderProc install size vs. the Codespace disk, and CPU render time per image. Whether a small labeled image set is achievable in one sitting. If not, decide on an optional GPU path via free academic credits, never a required one.
HOS 4Model calls per what-if sweep when an ADK agent is in the loop, vs. Gemini free-tier limits. A safety-envelope check that blocks unsafe actions before they reach the simulated device.
