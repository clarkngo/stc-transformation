Course Description

Discrete Mathematics and Data Structures in Computing bridges the theoretical foundations of discrete mathematics with hands-on data structures. This course emphasizes reasoning precisely about both mathematical structures and the programs that represent them in memory. Students will explore set theory and logic, using propositional and predicate logic to state and verify what a program should do, along with combinatorics, probability, and number theory as they apply to computing. The curriculum pairs these foundations with core data structures, including hash maps, linked lists, stacks, queues, trees, binary search trees, heaps, and graphs, and uses Big-O notation and recurrence relations to analyze their time and space complexity. Learners gain hands-on experience implementing these structures in Python and building graph algorithms such as breadth-first search, depth-first search, and Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm. Mastery of these concepts will equip students to choose the most suitable data structure for given performance constraints and to solve real-world networking and optimization problems.

Course Outcomes

This course will prepare students to:

O1 Articulate the mathematical properties of discrete structures (Sets, Sequences, Graphs) and demonstrate their representation in computer memory.

O2 Implement core data structures (Hash Maps, BSTs, Heaps) and apply Propositional and Predicate Logic to verify program correctness.

O3 Apply Big-O notation and recurrence relations to analyze the time and space complexity of algorithms.

O4 Evaluate and differentiate data structures to identify the most suitable option for given performance constraints.

O5 Design and implement graph algorithms (Dijkstra’s, BFS/DFS) to solve real-world networking and optimization problems.

Additional Information

Core Concepts & Tools

  • Propositional and predicate logic as program specifications: preconditions, postconditions, assertions, and loop invariants

  • Proofs, sets, and sequences, and how Python lists, sets, and dictionaries represent them in memory

  • Functions, Boolean algebra, and hash functions; hash maps with chaining, load factor, and resizing

  • Relations and digraphs; linked lists, stacks, and queues

  • Big-O time and space analysis; recursion and recurrence relations

  • Number theory in computing: modular arithmetic, GCD, primes

  • Counting and discrete probability, including hash collisions and expected vs. worst-case cost

  • Trees, binary search trees, heaps, and priority queues

  • Graph representations, BFS/DFS, and Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm

  • Python, with labs in zyBooks

Virtual Lab (VL) - Grading Criteria

Students complete labs that apply the math logic learned and practiced in HOS using a programming language (Python) to solve specific problems. VLs involve viewing instructional documents and following systematic instructions. Activities are embedded within each lab. The activities present a challenge to complete. Each lab is graded on accuracy and writing. A student has unlimited attempts at each lab to increase their accuracy and learn the required skills. Reports submitted include a write up on their understandings and findings in their lab reports.

From Module 3 on, labs that build a data structure (hash map, linked list, stack, queue, BST, heap, graph) also ask for the Big-O of each operation and a short timing comparison against Python's built-in equivalent.

Team Project (TP) - Grading Criteria

Project Description

The central task in computing is designing efficient computational methods, or algorithms, for solving problems. Choose a concept from discrete mathematics or a data structure and research how it is applied in real life. The instructor provides a list of topics. Students can suggest an alternative, but it needs to be approved by the course instructor.

The project includes a small Python implementation of the chosen concept or structure, a Big-O analysis of its key operations, and a comparison with at least one alternative structure or algorithm under the performance constraints of the real-life application (for example, a heap-based versus list-based priority queue in a routing service).

A team is selected consisting of three or four students. A group of fewer than three students require the instructor's approval. Each team chooses an instructor-approved topic relevant to the course.

The final project is the culmination of analyzing and evaluating required software, applied research, and instructor led activities throughout the quarter.

Students can work on a specific project, but it must be approved by the instructor and be relevant to the course. Each project consists of four elements: a proposal, a progress report, a final report of 6-7 pages, and a final presentation with slides. Templates are provided for each element by the instructor. Students add to their project elements weekly, incorporating feedback from their instructor.

Students are expected to use evidence to support the contentions they have drawn from their findings and critically analyze their cited resources. Resources should include assigned course materials and additional sources students have investigated and researched not assigned by the professor. Students use industry technical style of reporting and are expected to employ APA formatting for citations and references.

Team Project Assignments

Weeks 1-3: TP01 Team Project Proposal
During the first week, the Team Project will be explained to the students. The first submission will be a project proposal and is due at the end of Week 3 using the provided template in Brightspace.

Weeks 4-7: TP02 Team Project Progress
The second submission will demonstrate progress on the team project and will be due at the end of Week 7. The students will be provided with the final report template in Brightspace, and this assignment will ask the students to complete several sections of the final report. This will be explained in week 4.

Weeks 8-10: TP03 Team Project Final Report & TP04 Team Project Presentation
The final submission will include two documents: (1) The Team Project Final Report and (2) the Team Project Presentation. Both documents are due at the end of Week 10. It will be the instructor's judgment whether the final Team Presentation is performed live or voiced over PPT slides.

TP Report

Each student provides a report formatted based on a template provided by the instructor listing the team members. Students are required to improve their writing iteratively and incrementally every week. Students add new required sections to the existing paper every week.

The final report is the culmination of applied research and activities conducted throughout the quarter. The final report/paper provides a detailed problem and its solution. Students need to specify if the problem they are solving is likely to be encountered in industry.

Muddiest Point (MP) - Grading Criteria

Before class, students are required to submit the Muddiest Point (MP) activity to ensure understanding of discrete mathematics and data structures concepts. The purpose of this activity is to stimulate student engagement. The instructor uses the MP to assess how students understood the required readings. The instructor also uses the MP to customize the lecture scope to implement Just-in-Time Teaching (JiTT). The MP consists of writing a brief reflective essay (<= 50 words) identifying the most confusing part (i.e., the MP) of the content covered in the upcoming module. If a student understood all concepts, the student needs to explain the most exciting aspect. There is one multiple-choice question from the required reading to demonstrate that the student understood the required readings.

Concept Debate (CD) - Grading Criteria

The instructor poses a problem based on key discrete mathematics and data structures concepts introduced in the lecture. For example, how specific mathematical models are used by GPS to find the shortest route, or whether a hash map or a balanced BST is the better index for a given workload. After reflecting on the problem, students submit their responses, and the instructor reviews them without providing a correct answer. Students discuss their thought process and solution with a peer. Students then commit to an answer and re-submit their responses. Instructor reviews responses and thought processes with the correct answer.
