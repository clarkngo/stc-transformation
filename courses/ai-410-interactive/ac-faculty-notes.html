AC 1 — Cascade Family Clinic booking agent

Module 1 · Week 2Student page →Example answers →

The late-cancellation decision

Three common rules, all defensible. Grade the reasoning, not the choice.

  • Refuse inside 24 hours and send the patient to the front desk. A person decides whether to charge or waive the fee. The cost is friction for the patient. (This is the answer key's rule.)
  • Cancel and record the fee. Convenient, and the slot reopens for someone else. The risk is that a chatbot charges a patient money, and disputes go to the front desk anyway.
  • Ask the patient to confirm the fee first. The strongest answers notice the trap: a confirm_fee: true parameter is filled in by the model, so it isn't real confirmation unless the app enforces it outside the model. Students who spot this are showing exactly the "should the model control this?" judgment HOS 1 teaches.

Typical answers pick a rule without saying who it protects, or test only the easy case. The boundary is in the data on purpose: S06 is 23.5 hours away and S05 is 25 hours away.

The wish list

  • view_patient_record: strong answers don't expose it, or expose only the logged-in patient's own non-clinical fields. They point out that the record's notes hold health information the model could repeat back, and that the assistant isn't supposed to give medical advice anyway.
  • send_sms: strong answers reject a free-form (phone, message) tool, which would let the model text anyone anything, in favor of something narrow like send_reminder(slot_id): a fixed template sent to the number on file for the patient's own appointment.
  • Typical answers say "expose both, they're useful," or "don't, privacy," without naming what the model could actually do with the tool.

Grading quickly (about 8–10 minutes per student)

Each criterion maps to one part of the submission, so read each part once, in this order, and pick a level:

  1. Design (40): the pasted pytest output (all passing?), then test_my_rules.py (does a test sit at the 24-hour edge, using S05 or S06?).
  2. Application (10): the transcript. Did each message trigger the right tool, and did the P1002 session apply the student's late-cancel rule?
  3. Evaluation (30): memo section 1.
  4. Analysis (20): memo section 2.

You don't need to run the code. Load the table above into Brightspace as a rubric with levels (Full = 100%, Partial = 50%, Missing = 0% of the criterion), so grading is one click per criterion. A reference submission is in the answer key, with steps to check it against the provided tests.

Worth checking in the first term

  • Do a faculty test run in a fresh Codespace (AC 1 is the only challenge that calls Gemini live). Does it fit in 60–90 minutes with an AI assistant?
  • Do the memos actually differ on the late-cancel rule, or does everyone copy the same AI suggestion?
  • Does grading actually take 8–10 minutes per student? Do two instructors give the same levels to the same submission?
  • Is the transcript worth its 10 points, or should it become a single screenshot?

AC 2 — Riverbend help-desk retrieval

Module 2 · Week 4Student page →Example answers →

The open decision

Several rules are defensible. Grade the reasoning, not the choice.

  • Low threshold (about 0.15–0.20): answer almost everything. Lowest wait for students; accepts 4–5 wrong answers on this set.
  • Middle (about 0.25, the answer key): 10 right, 3 wrong, 4 declined. The closest call is out-of-scope x04 ("change my password on the Wi-Fi printer") at 0.28, which still slips through. Strong answers name it.
  • High (0.30+): almost never wrong, but declines 6 or more answerable questions into a two-day queue. Defensible if the student argues wrong answers cost more (a missed deadline).

Typical answers pick a number without using the sweep, or treat "declined" as free. The best answers say who bears each kind of mistake: a wrong answer costs the student, a decline costs the student and the help desk.

The analysis

  • Chunking misses (the vendor's, fixed by section chunking): q01, q08, q14, q15, where the answer sat in a 500-character slice cut mid-section or mixed with another section.
  • Vocabulary misses (left after good chunking): q09 "Xbox" (the article says "gaming consoles"), q05 "didn't start" (the article says "did not start"), q16 "largest" (the article says "larger"). Chunking can't fix these; real embeddings or synonyms would. Strong students say so, and may show it with --gemini.
  • A strong extra: noticing that adding titles or headings to chunks lowers the offline score, and explaining why (word-count dilution).

Answer-key numbers: section chunking (body only) scores hit@1 0.81 against the vendor's 0.56. A reference submission is in the answer key, with steps to check it.

Grading quickly (about 8–10 minutes per student)

Each criterion maps to one part of the submission, so read each part once, in this order, and pick a level:

  1. Design (40): the pasted pytest output, then test_my_rules.py (real questions? one near the threshold?).
  2. Application (10): REPORT.md. Does it beat 0.56 at k=1, and are the sweep and misses there?
  3. Evaluation (30): memo section 1.
  4. Analysis (20): memo section 2.

You don't need to run the code. In Brightspace, use the rubric above with levels (Full = 100%, Partial = 50%, Missing = 0% of the criterion).

AC 3 — Pacific Bean refund guardrail

Module 3 · Week 6Student page →Example answers →

The open decision

Several rules are defensible. Grade the reasoning, not the choice.

  • Low dollar limit (about $50–$100), the answer key uses $75: most home-customer refunds go through; equipment and wholesale go to a person.
  • Percentage of the order (for example up to 50%): scales with order size. Strong answers notice it lets a $1,240 order refund $500+ automatically unless the hard limit catches it.
  • Extra routing rules: queue any repeat refund on the same order (the answer key does; repeat small refunds are how a limit gets gamed), or queue every wholesale account, or every "not as described".

Typical answers pick a dollar figure with no criteria ("$100 seems reasonable"), or test only $5 and $1,000 and never the edge.

The analysis

  • Schema: "twenty dollars", 12.833, -18, "1001", "broken", approved: true. Strong answers explain the extra field is the dangerous one: a model that invents an approval flag is trying to authorize itself.
  • Lookup: the October order, the whole grinder (only $104 left), the friend's order. A schema can't know who owns an order or what's been refunded.
  • Person: the $1,240 espresso machine, the $155 stale beans. Strong answers name a call that passes everything, like the last one, a $104 refund because the grinder "doesn't grind fine enough for espresso", which is buyer's remorse labelled not_as_described.

With the answer key's rules, the replay ends: 1 refunded ($12.00), 3 queued, 8 rejected. A reference submission is in the answer key, with steps to check it.

Grading quickly (about 8–10 minutes per student)

Each criterion maps to one part of the submission, so read each part once, in this order, and pick a level:

  1. Design (40): the pasted pytest output, then test_my_rules.py (is the edge of the limit tested?).
  2. Application (10): REPORT.md. Is every recorded call handled the way the student's rules say?
  3. Evaluation (30): memo section 1.
  4. Analysis (20): memo section 2.

You don't need to run the code. In Brightspace, use the rubric above with levels (Full = 100%, Partial = 50%, Missing = 0% of the criterion).

AC 4 — Puget Pantry substitution observability

Module 4 · Week 8Student page →Example answers →

The open decision

Several rules are defensible. Grade the reasoning, not the choice.

  • Page only when an allergen was pushed to the customer (the answer key): O-502, O-505, O-506. Rare, can hurt someone, and someone can act now (call the customer, stop the delivery).
  • Page on any quality 0.0: the same orders today, but it would also page for "no substitute found", which isn't urgent. Strong answers notice the score lumps the two together.
  • Page on a slow model call (O-505, O-507 at 3,900 ms): usually weak. Slowness is a dashboard trend unless orders are failing.

Typical answers page on everything that looks bad (price jumps, slow calls, low averages), which is exactly what the lead said makes people ignore the pager.

The analysis

  • Which span shows what: allergen problems show in choose_substitute (what was chosen) plus the customer's allergies; the price jump shows in the score; the slow call shows in model_ms. One log line would show only "order processed" for all ten.
  • Strong answers point out that the push is sent before anyone could check: by the time the allergen page fires, the customer already has the message. That's a design flaw the trace reveals but can't fix.
  • What the trace can't show: whether the customer accepted or returned the substitute, or whether a cheaper, safe candidate existed (the span records the count, not the options).

The recorded day: 10 orders, 13 substitutions, 8 pushes, average quality 0.58 (three 0.0s, five 0.5s, five 1.0s). A reference submission is in the answer key, with steps to check it.

Grading quickly (about 8–10 minutes per student)

Each criterion maps to one part of the submission, so read each part once, in this order, and pick a level:

  1. Design (40): the pasted pytest output, then test_my_rules.py (is there a hand-built edge case?).
  2. Application (10): REPORT.md. Do the pages match the rule the student describes?
  3. Evaluation (30): memo section 1.
  4. Analysis (20): memo section 2.

You don't need to run the code. In Brightspace, use the rubric above with levels (Full = 100%, Partial = 50%, Missing = 0% of the criterion).
