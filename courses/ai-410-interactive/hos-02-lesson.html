Embeddings, Chunking & RAG

Give your HOS 1 agent the right few paragraphs of your own documents at the moment it answers — chunk, embed, store, retrieve, inject. Built and tested in Week 3; explained and extended by hand in Week 4.

Lesson overview

Outcome O212.5% HOS + MP + CD + AC

Your HOS 1 agent can reason and call tools, but it knows nothing about your business. Ask it how long Northwind Outfitters gives customers to return a tent and it will answer anyway — with a typical return window it has seen somewhere, stated confidently. This HOS fixes that by giving the model the right few paragraphs of your own documents at the moment it answers. That's Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): retrieve first, then generate.

Week 3 gets the pipeline working (chunk, embed, store, retrieve, inject) and tests it deliberately, including the bug where adding RAG quietly breaks the tool calling you built in HOS 1. Week 4 is about understanding why retrieval works: why the query and the documents are embedded differently, and whether you can add knowledge to the system by hand.

Anchoring analogy

HOS 1's consultant is still on the phone, and still can't see your filing cabinet. RAG gives you a very fast assistant who, the moment a question comes in, pulls the three most relevant pages out of the cabinet and slides them across the desk before the consultant answers. The consultant's skill doesn't change; what changes is what's on the desk. If the assistant pulls the wrong pages, the consultant gives a confident answer from the wrong pages — which is why most RAG bugs are retrieval bugs, not model bugs.

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Explain what an embedding is and why similar meanings produce nearby vectors, even with different wording. O2
  2. Justify a chunking choice (size and overlap) and describe what goes wrong when chunks are too big or too small. O2
  3. Design and implement a RAG pipeline: chunk → embed → store in a vector database → retrieve top-k → inject as context. O2
  4. Explain why documents and queries use different embedding task types, and what quietly degrades if they don't. O2
  5. Test whether retrieval is actually helping, including when the documents don't cover the question and when a tool should still be called. O2

Lecture: grounding answers in your own documents

≈ 70 min3 short partsO2

Three short segments. Pause after each and try the quick check. Anything that stays fuzzy goes in your Muddiest Point.

Part 1 — Why the model needs your context ≈ 15 min

An LLM's knowledge is fixed at training time and general by design. It has never seen Northwind Outfitters' return policy, and it can't tell you that it hasn't — it predicts a likely-sounding answer either way. HOS 1 gave the model tools for things it can't compute; HOS 2 gives it context for things it can't know.

The obvious fix is to paste every document into every request. That works for five short documents and stops working quickly: every token you send costs time and money, the context window has a limit, and a model handed fifty pages answers worse than one handed the right three paragraphs. So the real task is selection — deciding, per question, which few pieces of text the model should see. That's the context engineering you met in HOS 1, now done automatically.

Worked example

A customer asks, "Can I send back a tent I bought three weeks ago?" Without retrieval, the model says something like "Most retailers allow 14 to 30 days." With retrieval, the backend first finds the chunk from return-policy.md — "returns within 30 days of delivery … unused and in its original packaging" — and puts it in the system instruction. The model's answer is now Northwind's actual policy, including the condition about packaging that a generic answer would have skipped.

Part 2 — Embeddings: turning meaning into numbers ≈ 25 min

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents the meaning of a piece of text. Gemini's gemini-embedding-001 model turns any text into such a vector. Texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions, and we measure that with cosine similarity: close to 1 means "about the same thing," close to 0 means unrelated.

This is why RAG beats keyword search. A customer who types "sending an item back" never uses the word "return," but its embedding still lands near the return-policy chunk.

"Can I send back a tent?" ↔ return-policy chunkhigh "Can I send back a tent?" ↔ product-tents chunkmedium "Can I send back a tent?" ↔ shipping chunklow

Illustrative — the tent guide shares the word "tent," but the return policy shares the meaning.

Two settings matter in the HOS 2 code. First, Gemini lets you say what a text is for: documents are embedded with task_type="RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT" and questions with task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY". Why that helps is your Week 4 Analyze question — for now, notice that nothing crashes if you get it wrong. Second, EMBED_DIM, the vector length, must be identical at ingest time and question time, or the comparison is meaningless.

Quick check

If you embed the question "What's your refund window?" and the only document chunk says "returns within 30 days," would keyword search find it? Would embeddings? Why?

Part 3 — Chunking and the RAG pipeline ≈ 30 min

You don't embed whole documents. A document covering shipping times and customs fees produces one vector that's a blurry average of both — a mediocre match for either question. So you chunk first: split each document into focused pieces (about 400 words in the HOS 2 starter), and optionally overlap neighboring chunks so a key sentence isn't cut in half.

Notice the last box: your tools are still there. The most common HOS 2 bug is a system instruction like "only answer from the context; otherwise say you don't know." Applied broadly, it makes the model refuse to call calculate, because arithmetic isn't in the context either. Nothing errors — tool calls just stop happening. Scope the instruction to content questions and say tools are exempt.

Try it before the build

Open the RAG Pipeline Simulator. Ask the return question at chunk size 30, then at 10 with top-k 1. Then turn on the buggy prompt and ask "What's 25 times 4?"

Teaching note

Students tend to blame the model when an answer is wrong. Train the reflex early: first print what retrieve() returned. If the right chunk isn't there, no prompt wording will fix it. HOS 4's traces formalize this same habit.

New words this week

Embedding & cosine similarity
A vector representing a text's meaning, and the score (roughly 0 to 1) for how closely two vectors point the same way.
Chunking & overlap
Splitting documents into focused pieces before embedding them, with a few words shared between neighbors so nothing important is cut in half.
Vector database (Chroma)
A store built to hold embeddings and quickly return the ones nearest a query. Chroma writes to a local folder, like SQLite.
RAG & top-k
Retrieval-Augmented Generation: fetch the k most similar chunks, then let the model answer using them.

    Concept Debate

    Graded · part of 5% CD categoryDiscussion board · 3 stages
    This week's question

    When retrieval finds nothing relevant, should the assistant refuse ("I don't have that information"), or answer from general knowledge with a clear caveat?

    You'll argue the side your debate team is assigned, then argue the other one, then drop advocacy and synthesize.

    Your side this HOS

    Sides rotate each HOS. In HOS 2, Debate Team 1 argues Side B and Debate Team 2 argues Side A — the reverse of HOS 1. Find your team under Communication → Groups in Brightspace. Not in a team? Before posting Stage 1, count the posts on each side and join the side with fewer; if they're tied, take Side A. How sides are assigned →

    Side A — Refuse when retrieval comes up emptyTeam 2 this HOS

    If no chunk clears the similarity threshold, say so and point to a human or a source.

    • A made-up policy ("yes, we gift wrap") creates a promise the business has to honor or retract.
    • Users can't tell a grounded answer from a general one unless the system is strict.
    • Refusals are testable: an uncovered question should always produce the same safe answer.

    Side B — Answer from general knowledge, with a caveatTeam 1 this HOS

    Give the best general answer, clearly labeled as not from company documents.

    • "I don't know" to every uncovered question makes the assistant feel broken.
    • Many questions ("how do I waterproof a tent?") are general, and the model's knowledge is genuinely useful.
    • A clear caveat keeps the user informed without leaving them with nothing.
    Stage 1 · by Tue

    Initial position

    Argue your assigned side with one specific claim and one piece of evidence — a test question you ran, a sample doc, a simulator run. Not a topic summary.
    Stage 2 · by Thu

    Argue the opposite

    Before replying to anyone, post the strongest version of the side you didn't hold.
    Stage 3 · by Sat

    Synthesis

    Drop advocacy. State a real position, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post.
    Weak synthesis"Both sides make good points. It depends on the use case."
    Strong synthesis"Marcus showed that 'do you gift wrap?' got an invented yes in his build — that's a policy question, and I now think policy questions must refuse. But Dana's waterproofing example is general advice, not policy. My position: refuse on anything that sounds like a commitment (prices, returns, availability), allow caveated general answers for how-to questions. The hard part is telling them apart, which is a classifier problem, not a prompt problem."

    Grading: Participation — initial position plus response to at least one peer (50%) · Writing — quality of argument and reasoning (50%).

    Application Challenge — review someone else's RAG pipeline

    5% of courseEvaluate & critique — don't run it

    A campus IT help-desk team built the pipeline below to answer student questions from their knowledge base. It runs without errors. Your job: find one thing it gets right, one thing likely to quietly underperform (answer worse, not crash), and explain how you'd confirm your suspicion without running it.

    # helpdesk_rag.py  (someone else's code)
kb = chromadb.PersistentClient(path="./kb_db")
col = kb.get_or_create_collection("articles")

def ingest(article_id, text):
    for i in range(0, len(text), 2000):              # split every 2000 characters
        piece = text[i:i+2000]
        vec = embed(piece, task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY")
        col.add(ids=[f"{article_id}-{i}"], embeddings=[vec],
                documents=[piece], metadatas=[{"source": article_id}])

def answer(question):
    q = embed(question, task_type="RETRIEVAL_QUERY")
    hits = col.query(query_embeddings=[q], n_results=1)
    context = hits["documents"][0][0]
    return gemini(f"{question}\n\nAnswer using this: {context}")

    Warm-up: classify each observation

    Is each one something the code gets right, something that will quietly underperform, or a crash risk? Then reveal the reasoning.

    What to submit
    • One thing the pipeline gets right, and why it matters.
    • One thing likely to quietly underperform, with the specific question type it would get wrong.
    • How you'd confirm it without running the code — what you'd read, compare, or ask for.

    Grading: Judgment & reasoning (50%) · Transfer to the new pipeline, not a restatement of your own build (35%) · Clarity (15%).
