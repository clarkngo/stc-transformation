Lesson overview

Outcomes O4 & O512.5% HOS + MP + CD + AC+ Architecture Review & Integration CD

Three HOS in, your app reasons, retrieves, validates, and works in the background — and you can only see any of that from a browser tab and a terminal. This last HOS takes it to production shape. A mobile client puts the app on a real phone. Push notifications tell the user when background work finishes, even with the app closed. And observability lets you see what the agent actually did on every request, so "it gave a wrong answer" becomes a specific span you can open and fix.

Week 7 builds and tests all three. Week 8 is the last HOS week, and it does double duty: finish HOS 4, then step back and connect all four HOS in an architecture review and a final Integration Concept Debate — the bridge into your Team/Term Project.

Anchoring analogy

Think of a package delivery service. The mobile app is the storefront in the customer's pocket. The push notification is the "your package has arrived" text — sent to a specific phone, not just a name. And observability is the tracking page: every scan at every depot, with a timestamp. When a package is late, nobody guesses; they open the tracking history and see exactly which depot held it. A trace is that tracking history for one request.

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Connect a mobile client to an existing AI backend, and explain why the backend didn't need to change. O5
  2. Trace the push-notification chain end to end and diagnose which link failed when nothing arrives. O5
  3. Read a trace with nested spans and use it to locate a latency or quality problem. O4
  4. Evaluate the reliability of probabilistic outputs with a metric — faithfulness — and critique what it can and can't tell you. O4
  5. Explain the full path of a request across all 4 HOS and where it could slow down or fail. O4 O5

Lecture: production shape — mobile, push, and seeing inside

≈ 75 min3 short partsO4 · O5

Three short segments. Pause after each and try the quick check. Anything that stays fuzzy goes in your Muddiest Point.

Part 1 — A mobile client for the same backend ≈ 20 min

In HOS 1 you split the app into a frontend and a backend. Here's the payoff: adding a phone app doesn't touch the backend's AI logic at all. The Expo (React Native) chat screen calls the same POST /chat endpoint Streamlit did. You run it on your own phone through the free Expo Go app by scanning a QR code.

Two practical details. Your phone and your Codespace aren't on the same network, so start Expo with npx expo start --tunnel (the default LAN mode just appears to hang), and make the backend's port public so the phone can reach it. On iOS, Expo Go also needs you signed in to the same Expo account in the terminal and in the app.

Quick check

Your Streamlit frontend and your phone app both call /chat. Where does the Gemini API key live now, and did anything about that need to change?

Part 2 — Push notifications: a chain that fails silently ≈ 20 min

HOS 3 moved document ingestion into a background job — which means the user is no longer waiting when it finishes. A push notification closes that loop: "your document is ready," delivered even if the app is closed. It only works if every link holds:

  1. The app asks for, and gets, notification permission.
  2. The phone gets an Expo push token — an address for this device. (This needs a free EAS project ID: run npx eas init once.)
  3. The app sends the token to the backend: POST /register-device.
  4. The upload includes the device_id, so the job knows whom to notify.
  5. When the job finishes, the backend sends a message to Expo's push service, addressed to that token.
  6. Expo delivers it to the phone.

Break any link and nothing errors — the notification just never comes. Worse, Expo's push service returns 200 OK even for a stale or invalid token; the real error is inside the response body. And one platform limit: Expo Go on Android no longer supports push (SDK 53+). Chat works on Android, but to prove push, use an iPhone or a free development build.

Part 3 — Observability: traces, spans, and scores ≈ 35 min

Logs tell you that something happened. A trace tells you the whole story of one request: a tree of spans, one per step, each with its own timing, inputs, and outputs. In this HOS, Langfuse's @observe() decorator on run_agent() and retrieve() produces a trace where retrieval and generation are separate, nested spans:

run_agent
1.9 s
  retrieve
0.32 s
  generation
1.47 s

Illustrative. Real traces also show tokens, inputs, and outputs per span.

Timing is only half of it. AI output is probabilistic, so "did the request succeed?" isn't enough — you also need "was the answer any good?" That's what a score is: a number attached to a trace. This HOS uses faithfulness: is the answer supported by the retrieved context, or did the model add things? Scores accumulate across runs, so you can see whether quality is trending up or down after a change — the "appropriate observability tools and metrics for assessing probabilistic outputs" in Outcome O4.

Worked example

A user says the assistant told them Northwind offers gift wrapping. You open that request's trace. The retrieve span returned two chunks about packaging and shipping — nothing about gift wrap. The generation span's output stretched them into a policy. Faithfulness scored 0.67. Diagnosis in two minutes: a retrieval-threshold problem (HOS 2), not a model problem.

Try it before the build

Open the Push & Observability Simulator. Break the push chain one link at a time (try Android and a missing EAS ID), then open each trace scenario and answer "what do you check first?"

Teaching note

Budget extra support time this week — it has the most environment snags (Node missing from an older Codespace, Expo SDK mismatches, the Expo login requirement on iOS). The hands-on guide's Common snags list has a fix for each. Tell Android-only students up front that the push proof step needs an iPhone or a development build, so they don't lose hours debugging correct code.

New words this week

Push notification & push token
A message delivered to a device even when the app is closed, addressed with a token unique to that device.
Expo & Expo Go
A framework for building iOS/Android apps in JavaScript, and a free phone app that runs your project without app-store builds.
Trace & span
The full record of one request, and one timed step inside it. Spans nest: retrieval sits inside the agent run.
Score & faithfulness
A number attached to a trace to measure quality; faithfulness measures whether the answer is supported by the retrieved context.

    Concept Debate

    Graded · part of 5% CD categoryDiscussion board · 3 stages
    This week's question

    Should a production AI app trace and score every request, or only a sample of them?

    You'll argue the side your debate team is assigned, then argue the other one, then drop advocacy and synthesize.

    Your side this HOS

    Sides rotate each HOS. In HOS 4, Debate Team 1 argues Side B and Debate Team 2 argues Side A. Find your team under Communication → Groups in Brightspace. Not in a team? Before posting Stage 1, count the posts on each side and join the side with fewer; if they're tied, take Side A. How sides are assigned →

    Side A — Trace and score everythingTeam 2 this HOS

    Every request gets a full trace and a faithfulness score.

    • The one wrong answer a user complains about is exactly the one a 10% sample will have missed.
    • AI failures are rare and probabilistic — you need volume to see them.
    • Free tiers are generous at course scale; cost only matters later.

    Side B — Sample a fraction of requestsTeam 1 this HOS

    Trace and score a representative sample, plus every request that errors.

    • Every trace stores user questions and retrieved documents — more data kept means more privacy risk.
    • Scoring every answer (especially with another model) can cost as much as answering it.
    • Trends need a good sample, not a census.
    Stage 1 · by Tue

    Initial position

    Argue your assigned side with one specific claim and one piece of evidence — a trace from your build, a free-tier limit, a privacy scenario.
    Stage 2 · by Thu

    Argue the opposite

    Before replying to anyone, post the strongest version of the side you didn't hold.
    Stage 3 · by Sat

    Synthesis

    Drop advocacy. State a real position, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post.

    Grading: Participation — initial position plus response to at least one peer (50%) · Writing — quality of argument and reasoning (50%).

      Integration Concept Debate — connecting all 4 HOS

      Graded · CD categorySame 3-stage format
      The question

      Under a Team/Term Project deadline, which of the 4 HOS is safest to cut a corner on — and which one can't be shortchanged?

      Your side

      For the Integration debate, Debate Team 1 argues Side A and Debate Team 2 argues Side B. In Stage 3, your synthesis must name both the safest corner to cut and the one you'd never shortchange.

      Side A — Observability (HOS 4) is the safest corner to cutTeam 1

      • Users never see traces; a demo works without them.
      • Tracing can be added after launch without changing behavior.
      • Without guardrails (HOS 3) or grounding (HOS 2), users get wrong answers today.

      Side B — Never cut observability; cut async (HOS 3's queue) insteadTeam 2

      • Without traces, every bug in demo week becomes guesswork.
      • At project scale, uploads are small — inline ingestion is slow but tolerable.
      • You can't show a grader that retrieval works without evidence.

      Initial position

      Argue your assigned side using evidence from your own builds across the term.

      Argue the opposite

      The strongest version of the other side.
      Stage 3 · by Sun

      Synthesis
