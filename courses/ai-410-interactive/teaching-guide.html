🌫️

Responding to Muddiest Points

≈ 30 min per HOS
  1. Cluster, don't reply one by one. Most submissions fall into three or four groups. Tag each with its lecture part or file.
  2. Answer the top three in one post (or a 5-minute video) before students reach Evaluate. Quote a submission (anonymized) for each — students see their confusion was shared and specific questions get rewarded.
  3. Feed the rest forward. A cluster you couldn't answer well is a lecture-part rewrite for next term.

The lessons' specificity meter nudges students toward the four-field format, but grading stays with the rubric: participation — on time and genuine (80%) — and writing — specific, not generic (20%). A vague submission on time still earns most of the participation credit; the goal is better questions, not fewer points.

⚖️

Running Concept Debates

3 stages per HOS
  • Sides rotate automatically: Team 1 argues Side A in HOS 1, HOS 3 and the Integration debate, and Side B in HOS 2 and HOS 4. You never assign individuals. Late adds without a team join the side with fewer Stage 1 posts.
  • Stage 2 is the one students skip. Posting the strongest version of the other side before replying to anyone is what prevents "I agree" threads. Check for it first when grading.
  • Stage 3 must cite a classmate's specific point. A synthesis that only affirms agreement doesn't meet the Writing criterion.
  • Evidence from the build counts most. A test the student ran, a trace, a simulator setting — reward it over general claims.

✋

Checking that Understand & Refine was done by hand

40% of each HOS

The Refine stage is the mastery check, so it's where AI use matters most. AI-text detectors are unreliable and shouldn't be the basis of a grade. These checks are cheaper and fairer — pick one or two that suit your section:

  • One-line follow-up. Pick one line of the student's Refine code and ask them to explain it — a two-minute async video, a discussion reply, or live in office hours. Someone who wrote it can answer immediately.
  • A separate commit. Ask for the Refine work as its own commit (or small series of commits) after the scaffold. Small, incremental diffs with a clear message look very different from one large pasted block.
  • "One thing your version still gets wrong." Every Refine checklist ends with a limitation question (a bad input the schema misses, a case the faithfulness heuristic is fooled by). Students who built it by hand tend to know; generated explanations tend to claim completeness.
  • Spot live checks. In office hours, ask a student to make a tiny change to their Refine code (rename a field, add one validation rule) while sharing their screen.

State the policy in Week 1 and repeat it in each lesson — every Refine section is labeled "No AI assistant for this part."

Per-HOS notes: likely confusions and snags

Plan support time around these
HOS 1 — Foundations & the Agentic Loop (Weeks 1–2)

Likely muddiest points

  • Where the tool actually runs (many assume the model runs it).
  • Why the backend resends the whole history with every call.
  • Why the API key can't sit in the frontend "just for a demo."

Snags

  • First-time Codespaces and .env setup; a missing or mistyped GEMINI_API_KEY.
  • Streamlit and uvicorn both need to run — two terminals.

HOS 2 — Embeddings, Chunking & RAG (Weeks 3–4)

Likely muddiest points

  • Why documents and queries use different task_type values.
  • Why chunk at all, and how big.
  • Why the model still invents answers when retrieval finds nothing relevant (this week's debate).

Snags

  • RAG instruction suppresses tool calls — the most common silent bug.
  • EMBED_DIM mismatch between ingest and retrieval.
  • Forgetting to re-run ingest.py after adding the sixth document.

Lesson · Simulator · Hands-on guide

HOS 3 — Guardrails & Async Processing (Weeks 5–6)

Likely muddiest points

  • Why the worker must be a separate process rather than a function or thread.
  • Why the validation error goes into the retry.
  • Why a schema can't catch "valid but wrong."

Snags

  • Jobs stuck on queued — the worker isn't running. By far the most common.
  • macOS + RQ fork issue when running locally (use Codespaces, or the env var in the guide).
  • Retries that repeat the same error because the hint isn't fed back.

HOS 4 — Mobile, Push & Observability (Weeks 7–8)

Likely muddiest points

  • How nested spans are decided.
  • Why push is addressed to a device token, not a user.
  • What a faithfulness score can and can't detect.

Snags — budget the most support time here

  • npm missing in an older Codespace — rebuild the container.
  • Expo SDK mismatch with Expo Go — npx expo install expo@latest then --fix.
  • iOS Expo Go requires the same Expo account signed in on the terminal and the phone.
  • Push token crash without an EAS project ID — npx eas init.
  • Android Expo Go no longer supports push (SDK 53+). Tell Android-only students in advance: iPhone or a development build for the push proof step.
  • Expo push returns 200 OK for stale tokens — the error is in the response body.

Weeks 9–10: running the Team/Term Project

20% of course
  • Early Week 9: ask every team for its Sprint 1 list (the backlog builder copies it as Markdown). Check that Sprint 1 touches at least two HOS end to end, and that every HOS appears somewhere in the backlog.
  • Mid Week 9: read standups for "blocked" lines older than a day — that's where to spend office hours.
  • Early Week 10: feature freeze. Anything not working now gets named in the demo as "didn't make it," not rushed.
  • Demo day: require a backup recording or screenshots — tunnels and Wi-Fi fail live, and a network failure shouldn't cost a Presentation grade. Ask each team "show me where HOS 2 is" — it tests Synthesis & Scope in one question.
  • Collaboration (15%): teams submit individual contribution notes linked to commits or issues; solo students submit a process reflection from their daily log.
