What Weeks 9–10 are for

20% of courseTeam or solo

The project is the centerpiece of your portfolio: automate one real multi-step business process end to end, running from a single trigger, and prove how well it works with an evaluation report. It's synthesis — you built every technical piece in HOS 1–4.

The course description's examples both work as starting points: an autonomous marketing department (research → draft → brand review → schedule) or a self-healing coding assistant (read failing test → diagnose → patch → re-run → open PR). So does any real process that meets the minimum bar.

Day 1: map the manual process

The agentic mindset

Shadow or interview someone who does the process by hand, and list its steps. For each step, decide: agent (judgment, varying inputs), deterministic code (a fixed rule), or human checkpoint (a decision that needs a person) — and why. This is the course description's "agentic mindset" in practice. Human checkpoints are allowed only where a task truly needs one, and each needs a justification.

🗂️

Backlog and Sprint 1

Scrum-liteKeep it brief

Turn each step of your process map into a user story — "As a [user], I want [capability], so that [benefit]" — and tag the HOS it depends on. Mark a small, realistic subset as Sprint 1. Your list stays in this browser; copy it into your PM tool when it's ready.

Roles, standups and a PM tool

Teams: split by area, not by week — e.g. tools & MCP server, agents & orchestration, evaluation harness, and README/demo. Everyone commits code. Solo: keep a daily log instead of standups; it becomes your process reflection.

Build

Rest of Week 9 → Week 10
  • Start from your portfolio: your HOS 4 team and harness are the skeleton. Change what the process needs; don't start over.
  • Try a starter if it fits: the vendored ADK samples marketing-agency and software-bug-assistant in agent-development match the two example projects. Check they run on a plain Gemini key first.
  • Happy path first: one trigger → the whole process → a result, before widening any step.
  • Write eval scenarios early — a first draft of 15+ in Week 9 — and check whether a run fits the free tier's rate limits. Use a smaller model for the simulated user and judge.
  • Keep traces on. The demo needs one real failure trace from development: what broke, how it showed up, what fixed it.

Demo plan

Self-check and reflection

Self-check before you present: can you name where reasoning, planning, tool use, and memory each live in your project? Can you state your success rate, cost per task, and p95 latency from memory?

  • Teams: what you personally built (link commits or issues), one design decision you argued for, and one thing a teammate did that made the project better.
  • Solo: your daily log summarized — where you lost the most time, and what you'd scope differently.
  • Everyone: one moment where a trace or an eval caught a problem before the demo.
