Module 0 — Universal Tooling & Physical Basics

Before HOS 1

Two kinds of groundwork: the tooling every HOS uses, and a few physics habits — units, time steps, uncertainty — that software courses usually skip.

The groundwork under every HOS.

Docker Compose

Runs several containers together from one YAML file — in HOS 1, a message broker, a flow tool, a time-series database, and a dashboard.

Why it matters: A 2-core Codespace has limited memory; the feasibility test checks what fits.

Simulated sensors

A small program that publishes realistic readings — with noise and the occasional fault — in place of real hardware.

Why it matters: Real hardware is out of scope; the data connection is real.

Units

Every number has a unit. Mixing Celsius and Fahrenheit, or meters and feet, is the classic physical-system bug.

Why it matters: A twin acting on a wrong-unit reading acts wrongly with full confidence.

Time steps

Simulations move the world forward in small steps of time. Too large a step and physics becomes unstable or inaccurate.

Measurement noise and uncertainty

Every reading is approximate. Models should expect noise; decisions should leave margin for it.

CPU-only constraints

No GPU in a Codespace. Training and rendering are sized to finish in minutes; any GPU path is optional, never required.

Vocabulary check
Service
one running program in a Compose stack.
Time step (Δt)
how far a simulation advances per update.
Noise
random variation in a measurement.
Quick self-check before you continue
  • What's the unit of every number your system will act on?
  • Why might a simulation with a large time step misbehave?
  • What does "sensors are simulated" change, and what doesn't it?

Module A — Digital Shadows, Twins & Real-Time Pipelines

Before HOS 1Outcome 1Outcome 3

Why before you start: the difference between a shadow and a twin is one arrow — and that arrow decides how much correctness, latency, and safety matter.

A digital twin's three components.

Digital model, shadow, twin

Model: updated by hand. Shadow: updated automatically, one way. Twin: the virtual side can also act on the physical system.

Why it matters: Outcome O1 asks you to explain the difference.

The three components

The physical entity, the virtual entity, and the data connection between them.

MQTT

A lightweight publish/subscribe protocol. Sensors publish to topics (building/floor3/room12/temp); subscribers receive what they listen for (proposed broker: Mosquitto).

Time-series database and dashboard

Storage built for timestamped measurements, and a live view of them (proposed: InfluxDB and Grafana, with Node-RED flows in between).

Real-time is a number

End-to-end latency from reading to virtual model, plus data loss. A thermostat tolerates seconds; a drone does not.

Why it matters: Outcome O3 asks for pipelines that synchronize in real time — for your use case.

Sensor faults

Stuck values, impossible jumps, dropouts, clock skew. A stuck reading looks calm and normal.

Why it matters: A twin acting on a stuck sensor keeps doing the wrong thing.
Vocabulary check
Topic
the named channel an MQTT message is published to.
Publish / subscribe
senders and receivers don't know about each other; the broker connects them.
End-to-end latency
time from a physical reading to its effect.
Quick self-check before you continue
  • What one change turns a shadow into a twin?
  • Which sensor fault looks exactly like a real reading?
  • How would you measure end-to-end latency?

Module B — Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Before HOS 2Outcome 2

Why before you start: a twin needs predictions where no sensor is. A network fit only to data can predict physically impossible things there; a PINN can't, as long as its physics is right.

What a PINN minimizes.

Governing equation

The physical law a system obeys — the heat equation, a damped spring's m·x″ + c·x′ + k·x = 0.

Data-only models between the data

With sparse, noisy readings a flexible network fits the noise and does anything between and beyond the points — including the impossible.

The physics residual

How far the network's prediction is from satisfying the equation, evaluated at sampled points (collocation points). Derivatives come from automatic differentiation of the network itself.

λ, the balance

Weights the physics term against the data term. Too small: a data-only model. Too large: physics wins even when the data says your equation is wrong.

Boundary and initial conditions

Known values at the edges of space or the start of time the solution must match.

When a PINN is the wrong tool

When the equation is unknown or wrong, when there's plenty of data and no trusted physics, or when training on CPU takes hours.

Why it matters: Wrong physics makes a PINN confidently wrong.
Vocabulary check
Collocation points
where the physics residual is evaluated.
Automatic differentiation
exact derivatives computed through the network.
Residual
how much an equation fails to hold.
Quick self-check before you continue
  • What does the physics term add that more data can't?
  • What happens at a very large λ?
  • When would you not use a PINN?

Module C — Simulation, Synthetic Data & Sim-to-Real

Before HOS 3Outcome 4

Why before you start: simulation lets you practice the rare and dangerous — but only if what's learned there transfers. This is also the course's highest-risk unit on CPU.

From simulation to a measured sim-to-real gap.

Physically accurate simulation

A physics engine steps the world forward: masses, joints, friction, contact (proposed: MuJoCo, headless on CPU).

High-fidelity 3D scenes

Scene descriptions (proposed: OpenUSD) rendered into images (proposed: BlenderProc). How photorealistic a CPU can get is what the feasibility test decides.

Why it matters: Outcome O4 says "photorealistic" — on a CPU, that's a real constraint.

Synthetic data generation

Labeled training data from simulation: boxes, masks, and depth come free and perfect for every image.

Domain randomization

Varying lighting, textures, friction, and mass on purpose so the model learns what's invariant instead of the simulator's quirks. Too wide, and the task becomes unlearnable.

The sim-to-real gap

The performance lost moving from simulation to reality — measured here against held-out worlds with altered physics, since real hardware is out of scope.

Policy

The learned mapping from observations to actions (proposed: Gymnasium environments with stable-baselines3 on CPU).

Vocabulary check
Headless
running without a display.
Held-out world
a test environment the model never trained on.
Invariant
what stays true across variations.
Quick self-check before you continue
  • What does a synthetic label give you that a real one costs?
  • Why can randomizing too widely hurt?
  • How will you measure the gap without hardware?

Module D — What-If Testing & Safe Autonomous Decisions

Before HOS 4Outcome 5

Why before you start: the point of a twin is asking questions you can't safely ask the real system — and then letting an agent act only where those answers proved it's safe.

From what-if testing to safe autonomy.

What-if scenario

A simulation run with a failure or extreme condition introduced on purpose — an HVAC unit failing on the hottest day.

Why it matters: Outcome O5 asks you to predict failures before deployment.

Parameter sweep

Running many what-ifs systematically across ranges (outside temperature × equipment capacity × occupancy) to find where outcomes become unsafe (proposed: SimPy or MuJoCo).

Why it matters: Single-failure sweeps miss combined failures.

Safety envelope

Limits on states (room temperature 18–27 °C) and actions (setpoint changes ≤ 2 °C per 10 minutes) proven safe in simulation. An action outside it is blocked and a person is notified.

Why it matters: An envelope is only as good as the model and sensors it relies on.

An agent acting on the twin

An agent reads the twin and acts over MQTT, with the envelope check running before every action (proposed: Google ADK, with the check as a callback).

Action logs

Every autonomous action recorded with its inputs, decision, and reason.

Why it matters: The log is what lets you tell whether the sensor, the model, the envelope, or the agent failed.

Ethics in safety-critical domains

Who is accountable when the twin is wrong? Which decisions must never be made without a person? Physical harm can't be rolled back.

Vocabulary check
Envelope check
the test every action must pass before it reaches the device.
Combined failure
two or more things going wrong at once.
Accountability
who answers for an outcome, separate from which layer caused it.
Quick self-check before you continue
  • What does a parameter sweep find that a single what-if can't?
  • Why can an envelope give false confidence?
  • Who should be accountable for your twin's actions?
