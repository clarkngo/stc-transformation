Simulation, Synthetic Data & Sim-to-Real

Real-world data for rare or dangerous situations is scarce. Build a simulation that's physically accurate enough to generate labeled synthetic data and train a policy — then test whether it survives when the physics changes.

Lesson overview

O412.5% HOS + MP + CD + AC+ Team/Term Project checkpoint

You can't crash a thousand drones to collect training data. Simulation can — if it's physically accurate enough that what's learned in simulation transfers to reality. This HOS builds a physics simulation, generates labeled synthetic images, trains a small policy on CPU, and tests it against deliberately altered physics (the stand-in for reality, since real hardware is out of scope).

This is the course's highest-risk unit: the outcome says "photorealistic," and there's no GPU. The feasibility test decides how photorealistic a CPU-only Codespace can get. Week 6 ends with the Team/Term Project checkpoint.

Anchoring analogy

A flight simulator. Pilots train for engine failures there because nobody wants to practice on a real one. It works only because the simulator's physics is close enough to the real plane — and good programs vary the weather, the weight, and the wind so pilots don't learn the simulator's quirks instead of flying.

Learning objectives

By the end of the two weeks you will be able to:

  1. Construct a physically accurate simulation environment for a physical AI task. O4
  2. Generate labeled synthetic data from simulation. O4
  3. Use domain randomization to make what's learned transfer beyond the simulator. O4
  4. Measure the sim-to-real gap by testing against altered physics. O4
  5. Judge how photorealistic the data must be for the task. O4

Lecture: learning in simulation, working in reality

≈ 75 min3 short partsO4

Three short segments. Pause after each and try the quick check. Anything that stays fuzzy goes in your Muddiest Point.

Part 1 — Physically accurate simulation ≈ 25 min

A physics engine steps the world forward in time: masses, joints, friction, contact. Proposed tool: MuJoCo, headless on CPU. Scenes for images can be described in OpenUSD and rendered with BlenderProc, which also writes perfect labels (boxes, masks, depth) for every image — the main reason synthetic data is attractive.

Quick check

Synthetic labels are perfect. Why might a detector trained only on them still fail on real photos?

Part 2 — Synthetic data and domain randomization ≈ 25 min

Models trained on one clean simulated world learn its quirks: its exact lighting, its textures, its friction. Domain randomization varies those on purpose — lighting, colors, camera angle, mass, friction — so the model learns what's invariant. Too little randomization: brittle. Too much: the task becomes unlearnable.

Worked example

A box-picking policy trained with friction fixed at 0.8 succeeds 96% in simulation and 41% when friction drops to 0.5. Trained with friction randomized between 0.4 and 1.0, it scores 90% in simulation — and 85% at 0.5.

Part 3 — Sim-to-real, without the real ≈ 25 min

The sim-to-real gap is the drop in performance from simulation to reality. With no hardware, this course measures it against altered physics: change friction, mass, or sensor noise in a held-out test world and see what breaks. Proposed tools: Gymnasium environments with stable-baselines3 policies on CPU.

Try it before the build

Open the Sim-to-Real Simulator: set the randomization ranges, then test on worlds the policy never saw.

    Concept Debate

    Graded · part of 5% CD categoryDiscussion board · 3 stages
    This week's question

    For training physical AI, is synthetic data a substitute for real data — or only a supplement?

    You'll argue the side your debate team is assigned, then argue the other one, then drop advocacy and synthesize.

    Your side this HOS

    Sides rotate each HOS. In HOS 3, Debate Team 1 argues Side A and Debate Team 2 argues Side B. Find your team under Communication → Groups in Brightspace. Not in a team? Before posting Stage 1, count the posts on each side and join the side with fewer; if they're tied, take Side A.

    Side A — A substituteTeam 1 this HOS

    With enough randomization, simulation can stand in for real data.

    • Rare and dangerous events can only be practiced in simulation.
    • Labels are perfect and free.
    • Randomization covers variation real data never captures.

    Side B — Only a supplementTeam 2 this HOS

    Simulation helps; reality has to have the final word.

    • Every simulator leaves out something real.
    • Models exploit simulator quirks in ways nobody predicted.
    • Safety-critical systems need real-world validation.
    Stage 1 · by Tue

    Initial position

    Argue your assigned side with one specific claim and one piece of evidence — a run, a trace, a simulator setting. Not a topic summary.
    Stage 2 · by Thu

    Argue the opposite

    Before replying to anyone, post the strongest version of the side you didn't hold.
    Stage 3 · by Sat

    Synthesis

    Drop advocacy. State a real position, citing at least one specific point from a classmate's post.
    Weak synthesis"Synthetic data is useful but real data is also important."
    Strong synthesis"Ivan showed the 96%-in-sim policy collapsing at friction 0.5. Randomization fixed that case, but only because we knew friction mattered. So: synthetic data for training and rare events, a small real (or held-out altered) test set as the gate — the unknown unknowns are exactly what simulation can't randomize."

    Grading: Participation — initial position plus response to at least one peer (50%) · Writing — quality of argument and reasoning (50%).

    Application Challenge — plan synthetic data for a new task

    5% of courseDesign & justify — no build

    For a physical AI task this course hasn't built, plan the synthetic data: what to simulate, what to randomize and over what range, and how you'd measure the sim-to-real gap.

    Warm-up: randomize it?

    A warehouse robot must detect boxes on shelves. For each parameter, decide whether to randomize it, keep it fixed, or leave it out of the simulation.

    What to submit
    • What you'd simulate, and the labels the simulation provides.
    • What you'd randomize, over what ranges, and why.
    • How you'd measure the sim-to-real gap without the real robot.

    Grading: Judgment & reasoning (50%) · Transfer to the new scenario (35%) · Clarity (15%).
