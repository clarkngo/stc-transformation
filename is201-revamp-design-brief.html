Why: AI Ate the Bottom of Bloom's Taxonomy

The argument, in one ladder

Generative AI is now reliably better and faster than a first-year student at Remembering syntax, Understanding what a function signature means, and Applying a known pattern to a new but similar problem. Those are the bottom three rungs of Bloom's Taxonomy — and they are exactly what most intro-programming courses, IS 201 included, spend most of their grade on.

What AI cannot do for a student — because it requires human judgment tied to a specific context — is Analyze why a piece of code behaves the way it does, Evaluate whether a solution is actually correct, safe, and fit for a real business problem, or Create a solution to a problem that hasn't been fully specified yet. Those top three rungs are where a Python course earns its keep in 2026.

Higher-order thinking
Creating — design, build, formulate a solution Human center of gravity
Evaluating — judge, validate, choose between options Human center of gravity
Analyzing — trace a diff, debug, explain behavior Human center of gravity
Applying — implement a known pattern AI-accelerated
Understanding — explain, summarize, interpret AI-accelerated
Remembering — recall syntax, recite definitions AI-accelerated
The design principle for every decision below: the bottom three rungs stay in the course (students still need the vocabulary to read and direct code) but stop being the thing we grade heavily. They become the fast, AI-assisted on-ramp into the top three rungs, which become the center of gravity for both instruction time and assessment weight.

This isn't a new idea for STC — the AI Institute meeting notes from Jul 21, 2026 already floated a “flipped Bloom's Taxonomy” for CityUX's self-paced modules, and Peer Instruction & CATs in the AI Age makes the parallel case for in-class activities. This brief is that same thesis applied to IS 201's content and grading structure — and its specific AI-resistance mechanics (Debug the AI, Structural Reverse-Engineer, live time-boxing) are pulled directly into the new assessments below, not just cited by analogy. See Assessment Rebalance.

The Current Course, Diagnosed

IS 201's own course outcomes already span the ladder — its grading doesn't

The existing IS 201 Course Outcomes (from the current Course Outline) are, almost verbatim, a walk up Bloom's Taxonomy: CO1–CO2 understand, CO3 apply, CO4 analyze, CO5 evaluate, CO6 create. The outcomes were never the problem. The problem is where the 1,000 points actually go.

Current assessmentWeightBloom level it actually tests
Programming Exercise (PE)40%Apply (write correct syntax from a blank file, individually)
Hands-On Skill (HOS)20%Apply (follow steps for a specific tool)
Knowledge Check (KC)10%Remember / Understand (weekly quiz)
Muddiest Point (MP)5%Understand (reflective, low stakes)
Concept Debate (CD)5%Evaluate, in miniature (discussion-board depth)
Team Project (TP) — Tic-Tac-Toe20%Create (the one deliverable that reaches the top rung)

75% of the grade (PE + HOS + KC + MP) sits on Remember / Understand / Apply — precisely the work a general-purpose AI assistant now does at or above a first-year student's level, on demand, for free. Only the Team Project reaches Create, and it's a single generic exercise (a board game) with no connection to a real domain, no requirements-gathering step, and no evaluation of alternative approaches.

What the two new assessment types actually are

  • Diff / Debug Sprint (Analyze). Every module, students get a short commit history or before/after diff where an AI assistant made the change — framed as “Debug the AI”: find the fatal conceptual flaw or regression it introduced, not just narrate what changed. Run as a timed, synchronous (or screen-recorded) session, so leaving the room to ask a second AI costs more than just doing the work. This is the literal mechanism for “see code as diffs / commit history instead of typing it” from the original brief.
  • Solution Evaluation Brief (Evaluate). Students are handed 2–3 AI-generated candidate implementations of the same requirement plus an AI-generated evaluation of them — the task (a Structural Reverse-Engineer) is to find what that evaluation oversimplified or got wrong against a domain-specific constraint (e.g., a healthcare snippet handling PII, a finance snippet handling rounding), then recommend one in writing with their own reasoning.

Making these AI-resistant

Changing what an assessment asks for doesn't make it any less promptable — a Diff Sprint answer or an Evaluation Brief can be pasted into an AI just as easily as a Programming Exercise can. Peer Instruction & CATs in the AI Age already solved this exact problem for discussion-based work by shifting from evaluating the final product to capturing the live human process; the same mechanics carry over directly here.

Mechanic (from Peer Instruction & CATs)Applied in IS 201
Debug the AI — critique a flawed AI output instead of producing an answer from scratchDiff/Debug Sprint is “AI made this change — find the flaw it introduced,” not an open “explain this diff” prompt an AI could answer just as well
Structural Reverse-Engineer — given an AI-generated artifact, name what it oversimplified or got wrongSolution Evaluation Brief hands students an AI-generated evaluation and asks them to find its blind spot, rather than generate their own evaluation unassisted
Tight, live, unrecorded time-boxing — the friction of leaving to consult AI exceeds the friction of just doing the workDiff Sprint runs timed and synchronous (or screen-recorded), not an open-window async text submission
Capture the shift, not the artifact — evidence is how thinking changed, not just the final answerDomain Capstone's requirements step (Module 5) uses the Roleplay Feedback Loop: brief an AI playing a skeptical stakeholder, submit the interaction log showing how the requirements changed under questioning

Worked example — Diff/Debug Sprint

Module 6 (Files & Exceptions), Healthcare lens. Students get a 3-minute timer and this diff, framed only as: “An AI assistant made this ‘cleanup’ commit. What did it break?”

# commit: "simplify error handling" - except PatientNotFoundError: - log.warning(f"No record for {patient_id}") - return None + except Exception: + return None

Example submission (typed with 19 seconds left on the clock, screen-recorded):

“The except got widened from PatientNotFoundError to a bare Exception, and the warning log was dropped. Now a real failure — a database timeout, a malformed record — silently returns None exactly like a routine ‘patient not found.’ A clinician can't tell a missing record apart from a broken system, which is worse than an error in a healthcare context.”

How it's graded — fast, because a diff this small has only a handful of valid flaws to name, not an open-ended essay to interpret:

CriterionWhat's gradedWeight
Flaw identificationNames the specific change (widened except, dropped log) — not a vague “the error handling changed”40%
Consequence in domainExplains what breaks because of it, in terms specific to the healthcare framing, not generic “this could cause bugs”40%
Process evidenceSubmitted inside the time box; recording shows no second AI tab open20%

This is also the answer to the grading-consistency risk below: because each diff is authored with a small, known set of valid flaws, a TA can grade a stack of these in the time it takes to skim each one against the criteria above — no open-ended rubric judgment call required.

Net effect: the grade center of gravity moves from “did you type working syntax from memory” to “can you read, judge, and direct code toward a real problem” — and the new assessments stay hard to outsource to AI because the deliverable is a critique of AI output plus a live/logged process trail, not a clean final answer. Total still sums to 100%; no new point pool, just redistributed.

The Domain Switch

Click an industry; the example, dataset, and case study swap in place

Each module's higher-order activity (Diff Sprint, Evaluation Brief, capstone scenario) is written once per topic but authored in parallel across a small set of pilot domains. A simple tab control on the module page swaps the code snippet, toy dataset, and case-study framing — the underlying Python concept doesn't change, only the world it's applied to.

# Module 4 — Dictionaries & Sets, Healthcare lens patient = { "id": 4471, "age": 63, "readmit_risk": 0.72, } if patient["readmit_risk"] > 0.7: flag_for_followup(patient)

Pilot domain set

  • Maritime — MVP domain #1 — sensor/telemetry data, safety-critical thresholds, intermittent connectivity. Ties directly to STC's existing Blue Tech & Maritime initiative, so real case studies and subject-matter expertise are already on hand.
  • Marketing / E-commerce — MVP domain #2 — customer records, cart/inventory logic, A/B-test style data
  • Healthcare — patient records, appointment/readmission logic, data-privacy framing
  • Finance / Fintech — transactions, budgeting logic, rounding/precision edge cases

Maritime replaced an earlier Logistics placeholder specifically because it's not hypothetical — STC already has an active initiative and hub docs to draw from, which none of the other three domains have yet. Healthcare and Finance stay in the illustrative set (each forces a genuinely different constraint — privacy, precision) but are not built first.

Build note

Technically this is the same pattern as the AI 410 week-by-week guides and their starter-code/ sandboxes — a small client-side component per module, not a platform rebuild. Authoring cost scales as 1 core explanation + N domain variants per module, so the honest way to pilot this is Maritime + Marketing/E-commerce × Modules 1–3 as an MVP before committing content-authoring time to all 10 modules × the full domain set. Module 1 is live — see the IS 201 Week-by-Week Guides.

Known Risks

Where this brief is weakest — surfaced on purpose, not smoothed over

  • HighSkill-atrophy / cognitive loadCutting Programming Exercise from 40% to 10% may not leave enough deliberate-practice time to build the fluency that Analyze/Evaluate/Create actually depend on — Bloom's own model assumes the lower rungs are largely mastered before the upper ones are reachable. Mitigation: treat the 35/65 split as a hypothesis to pilot-test on a small cohort, not a number to lock into a syllabus untested.
  • MediumCurriculum-committee gate — deferred, not avoidedConfirmed: CityUX doesn't require curriculum-committee approval, so this pilot can launch on the design in this brief without that gate. But the actual credit-bearing IS 201 does require it — so this stays a real constraint on the eventual fold-back decision, not on the pilot itself. Mitigation: treat every module/assessment change here as provisional until a fold-back is proposed; budget committee lead time into that decision when it comes, not now.
  • MediumDiff/Debug Sprint authoring loadA fresh broken/working code pair is needed every module, times however many domains are live — a heavier ongoing burden than the domain-switch tabs flagged in the Domain Switch section. Mitigation: the same 2-domain, modules-1–3 MVP scope caps this cost during the pilot.
  • MediumGrading consistency for the new assessment typesJudging a Diff Sprint or an Evaluation Brief is harder to score consistently at scale than “does this program run” was, and no rubric or rater-calibration plan exists yet. Mitigation: reuse the competency → rubric → calibration pipeline already built in the Assessment Infrastructure Playbook instead of inventing a new one.
  • LowBloom's Taxonomy is a narrative, not a proofThe ladder isn't strictly sequential in the cognitive-science literature — real analysis often requires simultaneous recall, not recall-then-analysis in strict order. It's a strong stakeholder-communication device and a reasonable design heuristic; it shouldn't be cited as settled learning science if this brief goes in front of a curriculum committee.

Open Questions & Next Steps

What needs a decision before this becomes the mandatory docx set

  • Domain set — decided for the MVPMaritime + Marketing/E-commerce build first (Maritime for its tie to STC's existing Blue Tech initiative; Marketing/E-commerce as a low-context-cost contrast). Healthcare and Finance/Fintech stay in the illustrative four but aren't built until the MVP validates the approach.
  • AI toolWhich assistant do students standardize on for the AI-paired exercises (Claude, Copilot, something CityU-licensed), and does that choice need a procurement/compliance step?
  • Total point poolKeep 1,000 points / same percentage structure, or does the new assessment mix warrant revisiting the scale itself?
  • Dev environment — decidedRuns on GitHub Codespaces, browser-based, zero local install — continuing the direction already flagged in the Sep 1 tooling meeting notes ("validate local dev environments vs. GitHub Codespaces to find the smoothest student setup") and the click-to-launch Codespaces pattern CityUX's own execution plan already commits to for lab content. A real .devcontainer/devcontainer.json now lives in this repo; see the Week 1 guide for the pilot test of it.
  • PlatformDoes the domain-switch component live as a lightweight embed inside Open edX content blocks, or does it need CityUX platform team involvement to wire in properly?
  • Synchronous vs. self-pacedThe Diff/Debug Sprint's AI-resistance depends on it being timed and live (or screen-recorded) — but CityUX's usual model is self-paced, asynchronous Open edX content. Does this pilot need a live cohort cadence, or can screen-recorded async submissions substitute without losing the time-pressure mechanic?
  • Positioning — decidedLaunches as a standalone CityUX microcredential, not a for-credit IS 201 section — CityUX doesn't require curriculum-committee approval, so this design can move directly to build. What's still open: if the pilot succeeds, does it fold back into credit-bearing IS 201 later, and does that trigger a scaled-down version of this redesign (to survive committee review) or the full version as proposed?
  • Next deliverableOnce the above are settled: redraft the mandatory Course Syllabus, Course Outline, and Module Overview docx files to match this design, and adjust the Course Schedule as needed.
