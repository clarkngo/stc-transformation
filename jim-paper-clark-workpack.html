3. Proposed authorship criteria

Charter item due Nov 9 (both). A draft for Jim to react to.

Use the ICMJE four criteria to decide who is an author, and CRediT roles to record who did what. Both are widely used and easy to cite in a disclosure.

Who counts as an author (all four)

  • Substantial contribution to the conception, design, or analysis.
  • Drafting the work or critically revising it.
  • Final approval of the submitted version.
  • Agreeing to be accountable for all parts of the work.

How order is set

  • Log contributions in the decision log by CRediT role as the work happens.
  • At the Dec 11 checkpoint, the author with the larger share of conceptualization and writing goes first. If shares are equal, record that the authors contributed equally and agree the order.
  • Both authors approve the final order on Jan 12, as the charter says.
  • Conference '27 presenters can differ from the paper's author order.

5. Contrasting external case

For Clark's Dec 4 deliverable. Two candidates, one recommended.

The charter asks for at least one external AI-era assessment design that CEAU can be tested against, so reviewers don't see the case as self-confirming. A good contrasting case is published, used in practice, and answers the same problem differently.

AI Assessment Scale (AIAS) — recommendedUniversity of Sydney two-lane approach
What it isA five-level scale that sets how much generative AI each assessment allows, chosen from the learning outcomes.An institution-wide policy. Lane 1: secure, supervised, no AI (exams, vivas). Lane 2: open, AI allowed, and most assessments.
Unit of designEach assessment gets one permission level.Each assessment goes in one lane.
Contrast with CEAUThe AIAS decides how much AI is allowed. CEAU changes who does the work stage by stage within one assessment, with AI leading Create and the student leading the rest.Sydney separates secure from AI-allowed across assessments. CEAU puts both inside one task: AI-assisted Create, then a human-only mastery check.
EvidencePeer-reviewed framework (2024). A pilot at British University Vietnam reported fewer GenAI-related misconduct cases and more engagement with GenAI.Policy in force since Semester 2, 2025, and adopted elsewhere (e.g., Auckland). No published outcome evaluation found.
Sociomaterial readingTreats AI as a tool to be permitted or restricted, which is the separate-agency view the paper argues against. That makes it a sharp foil.Treats agency as separable by setting: human-only versus human-with-AI.

Why AIAS first

  • It's peer-reviewed and has pilot evidence. Sydney's approach is documented mostly on the university's own pages.
  • It works at the same level as CEAU (one assessment), so the comparison is direct.
  • It's well known to business educators, and the forum's audience will recognize it.
  • Use Sydney as a second, institution-level contrast in the boundary-conditions section.

Pre-set fit criteria (draft, to agree with Jim before Dec 4)

Write down in advance what would count as a poor fit for CEAU, for example:

  • An assessment where the scarce human contribution isn't evaluation (for example, a pure recall or skills check).
  • A setting where a human-only mastery check isn't feasible (very large classes, asynchronous with no live check).
  • A case where AIAS or the two-lane approach explains the design as well as CEAU with fewer assumptions.

Other cases found, if more are needed

Several 2025 course-level redesigns use oral defense to make understanding visible: a graduate machine learning course at Fairfield University (ASEE 2025, work in progress), a postgraduate machine learning module at Atlantic Technological University (ICERI 2025, abstract only), and a graphic design study of 38 students at Tamale Technical University. Evidence in all three is early.
