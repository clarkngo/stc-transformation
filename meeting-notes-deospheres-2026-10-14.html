Demos: Open edX, CityUX & Clark's Prototypes

What STC showed

Sam: Open edX MOOC pilot

  • Open edX (MIT/Harvard, 2012) running on DigitalOcean. Tested with Korean students over the summer.
  • Nonprofit example (DCMI): a 5-module leadership course for ministry leaders overseas (Cambodia, Nepal, Africa), built when COVID stopped travel. It has AI labs built with prompt engineering, video and audio lectures, a 3-month self-paced window, a student-worker TA, and two live "celebration" sessions with the lead instructor.
  • "Netflix-style" catalog: some courses free, some paid (compliance, for example). Sam's rule is to charge something even for a digital certificate, even just $1.

Clark: the CityUX ecosystem

  • Open edX, workshops, seminars, a community hub, YouTube, and GitHub all feed into official CityU programs. See the CityUX hub and the open ecosystem funnel.
  • Faculty model moving from research faculty, to industry faculty, to industry publishers: practitioners who already have content and publish it on CityU's platform.
  • Demos: the prompt builder for people new to AI, the maritime physical-AI explorer, industry primers, and the Vibe Coding showcase built by Korean students.
  • Clark's point: AI shrinks the coding work, so technologists must "move left" toward domain problems and asking good questions. Courses should build in domain knowledge, not generic examples.

Sam: the teaching approach behind it

  • Reverse Bloom's: learn by example first, then by doing, then the concepts ("let's dance first, then fix your form"). It targets non-traditional and lifelong learners who have little time, such as children of immigrants, who start behind in English reading.
  • Industry certificates as the backbone: AI fluency built on existing Google, Anthropic, and Microsoft certificates, re-taught for learners those certificates don't serve. Clark's Google AI certificate posted to LinkedIn was the example of the credential students want. See the teaching framework hub.
  • Domain expert as instructor: a 45–50-year-old tradesperson with no degree can teach on CityUX, with CityU supplying the platform and course design. "The key player is the domain, not AI": human in the loop.

Joneil's Thesis: Secure Local AI for the Trades

On-prem infrastructure, blue-collar learners, Everett manufacturers

The problem

  • Distrust of hyperscalers: regulated industries, small manufacturers, and Snohomish farms don't want competitive data in the cloud. Universities (Bothell, for example) have no on-prem AI sandbox.
  • Cost: blue-collar and immigrant students can't pay for frontier-model subscriptions. They should learn to evaluate and run open-source LLMs without depending on big tech.
  • Format: blue-collar learners don't like text-heavy courses. They learn on their phones between jobs, retention is low, and they drop out of COVID-style online courses. They prefer apprenticeship, learning alongside someone they trust.
  • Crowded market: YouTube, UW Bothell, UW Tacoma, and others all offer AI courses, and students can't navigate them.

The proposal

  • The library of the future: on-prem physical AI infrastructure (modular data centers) for universities, colleges, trade schools, and libraries, giving protected public access to LLMs.
  • Everett manufacturers: the City of Everett's economic development director shared a list of small manufacturers keen to train front-line staff on AI (smart factory, data analysis, automation): Formost Fuji, Eckstrom Industries, Lafarge & Egge, Hinds-Bock, and Achilles USA.
  • Owners want direct business impact: the apprentice brings real factory data into a private, secure local environment, runs scenarios, and the result goes into the business the next day, with the owner learning alongside.
  • Joint venture per company (about 6 months): CityU supplies the curriculum (physical AI plus AI theory). The company supplies apprentices and data. DeoSpheres supplies the infrastructure. Outcomes: a certificate, paid apprentices, revenue or cost savings, and possibly revenue share that lifts the apprentice's pay.
  • Regional collaboration: Noah Smith (UW) wants Seattle schools to advance open LLMs together instead of each building from scratch. The vision is to bring the best open models (UW's included) and CityU curriculum into these company environments.
Fit with existing work: this matches the corporate partnerships model (employer-facing AI enablement) and the hydraulic hose course prototype for blue-collar learners. The "too much text" criticism is worth acting on: a mobile-first, video- and voice-heavy version of the trades courses is a clear design gap.

Funding: Two Microsoft Routes

Short-term grant vs. long-term partnership

Microsoft Community First grant (short-term)

  • ~$20K, for King County nonprofits. CityU qualifies as a 501(c)(3).
  • One submission per organization, so it goes through an internal CityU competition. Due in about 10 days, and Sam is writing it this weekend.
  • Concept: AI fluency for the Korean American community. Content is built in English, translated into Korean, and self-paced, with in-person sessions at the start, middle, and end (at Hanin, for example). Funds would mainly support student-worker scholarships. Sam ran a similar internet-era class for a Tacoma church group, which his wife taught.
  • Sinae's advice: being a Korean American professor isn't enough. The proposal has to show direct partnerships with organizations in the community. She can authorize naming:
    • Greater Seattle Korean Association
    • Korean Community Service Center (Hanin 생활상담소). Sinae says it's better at bringing people together, and it's an easy connection.
    • Morning Star Korean Cultural Center
    These groups want new programs, and food plus education is enough for them to host. They won't charge much.

Microsoft Elevate (long-term)

  • Microsoft Elevate launched about 1–1.5 years ago: $5B to bring AI learning to 20M people through nonprofit and Microsoft partners, aimed at underserved communities and the Global South.
  • Sinae and Joneil think most of what CityU described fits Elevate better than the Community First grant, which favors immediate local impact.
  • DeoSpheres is pitching the same idea to Bellevue College (as part of its campus revamp) but says CityU "already understands it." Sinae: "Look up Microsoft Elevate, get the money, get the programming." Once CityU has a program UW wants, the UW relationship gets easier.
  • Sam will help with the Elevate connection.
Related history: CityU won AWS apprenticeship funding twice (~$0.35M, which funded Clark's earlier role). Large grants pay for staff; small ones prove the model.
