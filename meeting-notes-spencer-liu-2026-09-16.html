1. System Thinking After an Agent Error

The main idea driving the conversation
Core question
How does a team proceed after an AI agent returns an error, or a result that isn't what was expected? Spencer frames this as a systems-thinking problem, not a one-off debugging problem.
The role is now a blend
The role that handles this is a mix of PM and engineering — engineering now has decision-making authority that used to sit with product. Different departments across Google are being equipped with their own AI agents, which pushes this "what do we do when it goes wrong" judgment call down into engineering teams directly.
🗄️

2. Data Engineering — Metadata, Policy & Guardrails

Called out as a very important, currently under-resourced topic
Why it matters now
Data processing has become one of the most important topics in agent systems — specifically metadata, policies, and guardrails. This is squarely Data Engineering's territory, and it's the layer that determines whether an agent's mistake stays contained or cascades.

4. Agent Harness: Antigravity, ADK & Claude Code

Where each tool fits a student's or a team's workflow
Antigravity — for learning
Google Antigravity gives free users a generous token allowance. Spencer's recommendation: if a student is learning, point them to Antigravity instead of Claude Code — lower cost of experimentation while they're still building intuition.
Escalation path to ADK
Antigravity is positioned for generic tasks. When a request needs a customized workflow beyond what Antigravity can fulfill, the path is to move to Google ADK to build it directly.
Where it could live in the curriculum
Flagged as a fit for an Agentic Engineering, Agent Harness, or Agent System course. Spencer noted that at Google, all of their own systems are deployed in Antigravity.
Claude Code, by contrast: "pretty mature and complete" — less chance of something going wrong, which also means less opportunity to learn from working through a failure. The two tools were framed as serving different points in a learning arc, not as a straight replacement of one by the other.

6. Hiring Fresh Grads

Two interview archetypes, and the behavioral signal Google looks for
What fresh grads are evaluated on
How a new grad communicates with an AI agent, and specifically how they fix an error in an agent's result — this is treated as a real, assessable skill, not a soft skill.
Two interview types for the role
1. Model-area understanding — does this person know how the model builds something, i.e. genuine insight into how the model arrives at an output?
2. Application & integration engineer — can this person build and wire the systems that sit around a model?
The behavioral signal
What Google looks for in a new grad's interview behavior: curiosity and awareness about the agent's output and what to do next — a broader form of prompt engineering than just writing a good prompt. Spencer noted Antigravity may actually help surface this, since it needs more active attention to work properly than a more "finished" tool would.
🛰️

7. Forward Deployed Engineer — Multi-Agent Evaluation

How the FDE role checks agent work before it ships
Comparative execution
Forward Deployed Engineers run the same plan across different AI agents to see how execution diverges between them.
Agent-swarm decision-making
A group of agents will discuss and reach a decision together — described as an "agent swarm" pattern rather than a single agent working alone.
Verification before shipping
Code quality gets evaluated before it ships; verification includes actually running the app or website, not just reading the diff. Limits and constraints are built into that evaluation step.
🔬

8. Spencer's Own Research

Device computation and deterministic safety over non-deterministic models
Device computation
Same broad area as physical AI — models running on-device rather than in the cloud, and small models built for a specific job rather than one large general-purpose model.
Deterministic vs. non-deterministic safety
Spencer's framing question: how do you put a deterministic safety guard around a non-deterministic model? A non-deterministic system doesn't inherently have a safeguard for preventing behavior when its output has to be translated into a deterministic action.
Example 1 — speech to text: a model transcribing profanity or flagged language doesn't have a built-in guard for why that content can't simply pass through as-is; the non-deterministic transcription step and the deterministic "can this be output" policy step aren't naturally connected.
Example 2 — a robot washing dishes: if someone nearby starts yelling, the robot should recognize that as a trigger to stop its current task and check on the person — a deterministic safety response layered on top of a non-deterministic perception model.
