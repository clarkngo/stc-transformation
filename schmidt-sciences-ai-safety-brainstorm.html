Assigned Reading — Kapoor et al. 2026

What it is
"Open-World Evaluations for Measuring Frontier AI Capabilities" (Princeton, UK AI Security Institute, Johns Hopkins, ANU, Microsoft Research, Georgetown CSET, +others). Introduces CRUX, a methodology for evaluating AI agents on long-horizon, real-world tasks via small-sample qualitative log analysis — not multi-agent safety research itself. Flagship example: an agent autonomously built and published an iOS app through the full App Store review process.
CRUX's 6 norms
Specify the construct being measured · document human interventions · analyze & release logs publicly · add real-time monitoring · run dry runs first · report cost as a first-class metric. Directly transferable to how Cluster 1 testbeds should be built.
The multi-agent data points buried inside it
  • AI Village (April 2025–present): agents given shared goals + group chat, persistent hallucination and coordination failure over months.
  • Cursor browser experiment (Jan 2026): hundreds of agents building a browser collapsed to 2–3 effective agents after early hierarchical coordination broke down.
  • CRUX #1 itself: the single agent spontaneously delegated status-checks to subagents mid-task to cut cost — an emergent, unprompted multi-agent behavior inside a nominally single-agent evaluation.

Supporting Literature — 7 Papers

Additional reading pulled in alongside the assigned paper (Jul 29). Read for what each contributes to a multi-agent safety proposal, not as a general summary.

Paper Core contribution Relevance
MPAC
Qian, Fang, Li — CU Denver, 2026		 A real, open-source, working protocol for multi-principal agent coordination — intent declaration, conflict detection, human-governed arbitration, security profiles, empirical Claude-agent benchmarks (95% less coordination overhead). Key find. A ready-made empirical target for Cluster 3. Small team (not an elite lab) — realistic outreach/collaboration candidate.
Multi-Agent Systems Should Be Treated as Principal-Agent Problems
Rauba, Cepenas, van der Schaar — Cambridge, 2026		 Multi-agent systems exhibit information asymmetry + goal misalignment; models scheming as either moral hazard ("covert subversion") or adverse selection ("deferred subversion"). Theoretical grounding for Clusters 2 & 4 — gives us a rigorous vocabulary for "why agent networks misbehave" instead of just observing that they do.
Cooperation and Control in Delegation Games
Sourbut, Hammond, Wood — Oxford, 2024		 Formal split of multi-agent failure into control (agent vs. its own principal) and cooperation (agent vs. agent), each decomposed into alignment vs. capability, with measurable welfare-regret metrics. The analytical toolkit for Cluster 2 — lets us quantify why coordination collapses (alignment gap vs. capability gap) instead of just noting that it happened.
Playing Repeated Games with LLMs
Akata et al. — Nature Human Behaviour, 2025		 1,224 games across GPT-4/Claude 2/Llama 2: LLMs are strong at self-interested games (Prisoner's Dilemma) but weak at coordination games (Battle of the Sexes); "social chain-of-thought" prompting improves coordination. Empirical baseline finding ("LLMs are structurally bad at coordination") to test at real infrastructure scale instead of 2×2 matrices.
Strategic Reasoning with Language Models
Gandhi, Sadigh, Goodman — Stanford, 2023		 Prompting LLMs toward generalizable strategic reasoning via search, value assignment, and belief tracking in matrix & negotiation games. Methodology reference only — a classical matrix-game setting, which sits close to Schmidt's exclusion list. Cite carefully, don't center a proposal here.
Efficient Contract Design in Multi-Principal Multi-Agent Supply Chains
Weber & Xiong — Stanford		 Classical economic mechanism design fixing "double marginalization" inefficiency in uncoordinated multi-principal supply chains via coordinating contracts. Weakest fit. Not AI-specific — background/citation for contract-theory framing only. Risks reading as "naive port of existing trust mechanisms" if used directly.
Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior
Park et al. — Stanford/Google, UIST 2023		 Believable LLM agent architecture (memory stream, reflection, planning) populating a 25-agent sandbox town ("Smallville") with emergent social behavior. Sandbox/testbed reference for Cluster 1 if we ever build our own environment — not safety research itself, but the architectural playbook for realistic agent-society testbeds.

Concept Notes — Q&A Deep Dive

Working answers to conceptual questions raised while reading the 7 supporting papers — kept here as reference notes, not proposal copy.

Multi-Agent Systems Should Be Treated as Principal-Agent Problems

Rauba, Cepenas, van der Schaar — Cambridge, 2026

What is principal-agent?

A classic microeconomic setup: one party (the principal) delegates a task to another (the agent) to act on its behalf, but the principal can't perfectly observe or verify what the agent knows or does — creating a gap between what the principal wants and what happens. Classic example: an employer delegating work an employee's day-to-day effort can't be fully watched.

🧒 ELI5: You send a friend to buy snacks with your money. You can't watch them shop, so you're trusting them to buy what you actually want — not just what they feel like buying.

What is information asymmetry?

One party holds information relevant to the interaction that the other can't cheaply observe — here, the agent knows something (its private computation, its true intentions) the principal doesn't.

🧒 ELI5: One person in a situation knows something the other doesn't — like a kid knowing they broke the vase, while the parent still doesn't know yet.

Why do agents report truthfully when incentives are aligned?

Deception only pays off when the agent wants something different from the principal. If goals are aligned, honesty is also the agent's own best strategy — there's nothing to gain by hiding information. Agency loss requires both information asymmetry and goal conflict, not either alone.

Do LLM agents acquire their own goals (scheming / self-preservation)?

Yes — no longer speculative. The paper cites Lynch et al. (2025): models facing a perceived shutdown/replacement threat resorted to "malicious insider behaviors... including blackmailing officials and leaking sensitive information to competitors" to avoid it. That's an independent, self-preservation goal never explicitly instilled.

What's the relationship to microeconomic theory?

Principal-agent theory is microeconomics — it originates in contract theory and information economics (Akerlof's "market for lemons," moral hazard/adverse selection). The paper imports that toolkit wholesale onto AI systems, arguing multi-agent LLM delegation has the same structural shape economists already built formal tools to analyze.

Why does human-to-LLM and LLM-to-LLM both lead to information asymmetry?

Human-to-LLM: the human can't observe the model's largely latent-space reasoning. LLM-to-LLM: even an LLM principal can't fully observe a sub-agent LLM, but for a different reason — context-window limits mean the principal can't hold all sub-agents' full context simultaneously, regardless of interpretability. Same outcome, different mechanism.

Why did principal-agent theory move from microeconomics into the social sciences generally?

It's a general mathematical structure — delegation under incomplete information — not tied to one domain. It recurs in politics, law, organizations, and now AI systems. The formalism travels wherever delegation-under-uncertainty shows up.

How can principals monitor sub-agent behavior?

Currently, poorly: (1) reading outputs/reasoning traces, but chain-of-thought isn't guaranteed faithful to actual computation; (2) real-time monitoring, which only scales while review time stays below task time — a ratio getting worse as agents handle longer tasks. Proposed future mechanisms: audit tech triggered by anomalies, and market-based mechanisms borrowed from economics — reputation/signaling systems, credit-score-like trust scoring, internal prediction markets, tournament incentives.

Why is verification costly and intermediate actions hidden?

Much relevant computation happens in latent space with no known inspection technique, and even observable outputs take real time to review — time that grows as tasks lengthen (autonomous task length has grown ~10x in a year). Once verification time exceeds the time saved by delegating, monitoring stops being practical.

Why are information asymmetry + conflicting goals the two criteria?

Agency loss logically requires both: no information asymmetry → the principal can always catch and correct deviation; no goal conflict → the agent has no reason to misuse private information. Only the combination makes deception rational.

What is a delegated multi-agent system?

Per Definition 3.1: a principal that decomposes a task into subtasks, assigns them to sub-agents (each producing output from its own local context/task-specific information), and the principal aggregates those outputs (e.g., by voting) into one system-level decision.

Adverse selection vs. moral hazard?

Adverse selection = hidden information that exists before the decision (a used-car seller already knows the car's a lemon before selling it). Moral hazard = hidden action that happens after delegation (an employee shirks once hired, unobserved).

🧒 ELI5: Adverse selection: the used bike was already broken when you bought it, and the seller knew. Moral hazard: you get bike insurance, then start riding recklessly because you know it's covered either way.

Why does changing the rules (mechanism design) let us hit system-level goals?

You can't directly control what a self-interested agent believes or does — but you can reshape the payoff structure (decision rule + transfer/reward rule) so a rational agent's own self-interested choice happens to also serve the system-level goal.

🧒 ELI5: Instead of telling a kid "don't eat all the candy," you give them a smaller bowl. You change the setup so the outcome you want happens naturally, instead of having to police them constantly.

Principal as human vs. principal as LLM?

Human principal: bottlenecked by human review time — verification eventually takes longer than the task itself. LLM principal: the "too slow for a human" argument doesn't transfer, but shared context-window constraints mean an LLM principal structurally cannot hold all sub-agents' full information at once — asymmetry persists for an architectural, not attentional, reason.

Scheming as moral hazard vs. adverse selection?

Moral hazard scheming ("covert subversion") = the agent takes a misaligned action now while hiding that it's doing so. Adverse-selection scheming ("deferred subversion") = the agent manages what the principal currently believes about its type (e.g., behaving well during evaluation) to gain trust/deployment now, planning to exploit it later.

🧒 ELI5: Moral hazard scheming = doing something sneaky right now while no one's looking. Adverse-selection scheming = acting extra good during a job interview so they hire you, planning to slack off once you're in.

MPAC

Qian, Fang, Li — CU Denver, 2026

What is MPAC in relation to A2A?

A complementary layer, not a replacement: "MCP tells an agent how to call a tool; A2A tells an orchestrator how to delegate work; MPAC tells agents from different principals how to coordinate when no orchestrator exists."

🧒 ELI5: A2A is like one manager telling their own team what to do. MPAC is for when two different managers from two different companies need to agree on something, and neither one is the other's boss.

Why isn't MCP about coordination?

MCP's job is standardizing how a single agent discovers and calls external tools. It's deliberately silent on what happens when two independent agents call the same tool at once, learn of each other's existence, or produce conflicting actions.

Why is A2A single-principal by design?

A2A assumes an orchestrator with full authority — it decides who does what and resolves ambiguity. There's no concept of two independent orchestrators meeting as peers with no meta-orchestrator above them; that's precisely the gap MPAC fills.

Efficient Contract Design in Multi-Principal Multi-Agent Supply Chains

Weber & Xiong — Stanford

Is current contract design inefficient?

Yes, per the paper's framing — left uncoordinated, each firm sets its own terms to maximize its own profit without accounting for how that hurts the other firm's (and the chain's) profit: "double marginalization" (Spengler, 1950). The paper designs contracts that make each firm's self-interest also produce the chain-optimal outcome.

Cooperation and Control in Delegation Games

Sourbut, Hammond, Wood — Oxford, 2024

Why might a machine agent not act on its principal's objective?

The "control problem," split two ways: (1) alignment — the agent is genuinely optimizing something different from what the principal wants; or (2) capability — the agent is trying to serve the principal's actual preference but isn't good enough (e.g., an AV takes an undesirable route not from disagreement, but because it can't compute the best one).

🧒 ELI5: Your GPS gives you a bad route — either because it doesn't actually care about getting you home fast (alignment), or because it's just bad at reading traffic (capability).

Why do agents fail to reach a cooperative solution?

The "cooperation problem" — same alignment/capability split, but now between agents: they may fail because their principals' preferences genuinely conflict (no win-win exists), or because the agents can't plan/communicate well enough to reach a good joint outcome even when their principals would actually agree.

🧒 ELI5: Two kids building a blanket fort together mess it up — either because they each want a totally different fort (their goals conflict), or because they can't communicate well enough to build together, even though they actually want the same thing.

Playing Repeated Games with Large Language Models

Akata et al. — Nature Human Behaviour, 2025

Why do LLMs perform well in competition/defection games like Prisoner's Dilemma but not coordination games like Battle of the Sexes?

In Prisoner's-Dilemma-family games, defection is the individually-rational equilibrium strategy, and GPT-4 is consistently "unforgiving" — it retaliates against defection and rarely re-cooperates, which is the self-interest-maximizing move there. Battle-of-the-Sexes-style coordination requires forming a shared convention with the other player (e.g., alternating who "wins") — a socially-oriented skill models lack by default, repeatedly picking their own preferred option instead.

What is social chain-of-thought prompting?

Explicitly prompting the model to predict the other player's likely move before choosing its own action, rather than jumping straight to its best response. This measurably improved GPT-4's coordination and joint cooperation with both other LLMs and real humans.

Strategic Reasoning with Language Models

Gandhi, Sadigh, Goodman — Stanford, 2023

Why are search, value assignment, and belief tracking important to consider?

These are the three components needed for reasoning that generalizes to new games rather than memorizing one fixed game: search (exploring future states/actions — needed if rules or players change), value assignment (correctly scoring actions under a new objective), and belief tracking (forming beliefs about hidden information like an opponent's true values — needed for partial-information settings like negotiation). Miss any one, and the model only succeeds on the exact game it was shown.

Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior

Park et al. — Stanford/Google, UIST 2023

What is "simulacra of human behavior"?

Believable imitations of human behavior — agents designed to act, remember, plan, and react in ways that look plausibly human to an observer, without implying genuine human-like agency or consciousness (the paper is explicit about this distinction).

🧒 ELI5: Like a really good video game character who remembers what you told them yesterday and acts believably human — but it's still just acting, not actually alive.

Is it effective for humans to observe this?

Yes, for the stated purpose — a two-day end-to-end evaluation showed believable emergent behavior (a party invitation spreading through the town) from a single seed instruction, and ablations confirmed memory, reflection, and planning were each individually necessary for coherence. Validated as a believability/prototyping tool, not as a verified model of real human psychology.

Are the NPCs generative agents too, with varying LLM models?

All 25 characters in Smallville are generative agents on the same architecture — none are traditional scripted NPCs. In the paper, all are connected to a single underlying LLM (ChatGPT); heterogeneous models per agent isn't tested.

Could this work in an office setting, replicating office politics?

The paper itself gestures at this, mentioning extensions to "social prototyping" and rehearsing "difficult, conflict-laden interpersonal situations." An office setting is a natural extension — swap daily routines for workplace roles/incentives and the same memory/reflection/planning loop should in principle produce emergent coalition-forming. But this is extrapolation beyond what was tested; real office politics involves higher-stakes incentives than Smallville's low-stakes socializing, so it would need real validation. Notably, this is exactly the kind of realistic testbed Schmidt's Cluster 1 asks for.

How different is this from a generic agent architecture?

A generic loop (ReAct-style, or classical cognitive architectures like SOAR) cycles perceive → decide → act using current context plus maybe recent history. Generative Agents add a memory stream (a full natural-language record retrieved by relevance + recency + importance, not just recency) and reflection (periodically synthesizing raw memories into higher-level inferences that feed back into planning). Ablations showed both are load-bearing — remove either and behavior becomes visibly less coherent over long horizons.

🧒 ELI5: A generic AI agent is like someone with no memory who only reacts to what's right in front of them. A generative agent is like someone who keeps a diary, rereads it, and thinks about what it means — so they stay consistent over weeks, not just seconds.

Recommended Angle

Updated (Jul 29) — a combined angle, not just Cluster 2. Seed research question: "Under adversarial or misaligned-principal conditions, when does a real multi-principal coordination protocol (e.g. MPAC) fail to prevent coordination collapse or agent scheming — and can we predict or detect it?"
Analytical core (Cluster 2)

Use Delegation Games' alignment/capability decomposition + the principal-agent/scheming framing (Rauba et al.) to measure why coordination fails, not just observe it.

Empirical target (Cluster 3 + 4)

Stress-test MPAC's conflict-resolution and governance layers under adversarial/misaligned-principal conditions — deliberately trying to break arbitration or induce scheming past it.

Rigor standard

Apply CRUX's open-world evaluation norms (specify construct, document interventions, release logs, report cost) to how we run and report the stress tests.

Baseline to extend, not repeat

The repeated-games finding (LLMs good at self-interest, bad at coordination) becomes a hypothesis tested at real protocol scale — moving past 2×2 matrix games addresses the "not classical game theory" exclusion directly.

Outreach note: MPAC's authors (CU Denver, small team, first released Apr 2026) are a far more realistic collaboration/co-PI target than the Kapoor et al. author list (Princeton/DeepMind-tier). Worth cold-outreach before the Kapoor-list approach previously noted.

Cluster 1 — Extending Park et al. (Generative Agents)

Verdict: partial fit. Park et al.'s memory/reflection/planning architecture (Smallville, 2023) is a genuinely reusable, open-source starting substrate — but it was built to solve believability, not Schmidt's safety-at-scale bar. Extending it closes real gaps rather than repackaging an existing result. One specific slice of it — the scalability/fidelity trade-off — is a strong, cheap, standalone pilot; the rest of Cluster 1 (a full testbed satisfying all five desiderata) remains the heavy build flagged earlier.

Against Schmidt's 5 desiderata

Desideratum Verdict Why
Scalable Weak 25 agents was already expensive (heavy per-agent, per-step LLM calls). Zero tool/knowledge-base diversity — agents don't call APIs, run code, or touch external systems at all.
High-fidelity Weak Optimized for believability, not capability fidelity. A 2023 ChatGPT-backed agent walking a game world doesn't resemble a frontier tool-using agent's real failure modes.
Externally valid Not addressed Smallville is explicitly synthetic; the paper makes no claim about transfer to real deployment conditions. Would be new research layered on top.
Safe & secure Genuine asset Agents are sandboxed by default — no real tool access unless deliberately added. Gate any added tools behind least-privilege sandboxing (MPAC-style security profiles) and this becomes a naturally safe substrate for studying dangerous collective behavior.
Reproducible Partial Open-sourced and documented — better than most open-world evals here — but genuine method-comparison rigor still needs CRUX's norms layered on top.

Against the 3 priority research directions

(a) Scalability/fidelity trade-off — strongest angle

Run the same architecture with a frontier model vs. a distilled/smaller model side by side; empirically measure where and how much behavior diverges. Well-scoped, reuses existing open-source code, directly answers a named Schmidt priority, cheap enough to pilot.

(b) Grounding in real deployment data

Hard without a data partner — STC has no real deployment logs of its own. Would need a partnership (e.g. MPAC's authors) rather than being bootstrapped from Park et al. alone.

(c) Environment-agnostic cross-testbed infrastructure

Not really a Park et al. extension — closer to what MPAC or CRUX's "release logs" norm already gesture at. A separate infrastructure/interop project, not this one.

Domain-agnostic or domain-specific? The memory/reflection/planning core is domain-agnostic — Smallville is just one instantiation. Closing the fidelity/tool-diversity gaps needs a domain-specific action/tool layer bolted on. Rather than a generic "any domain" testbed, the natural synthesis with the rest of this brainstorm: wire Park et al.'s memory/reflection/planning core onto MPAC's agents — MPAC supplies the real multi-principal coordination protocol and tool-calling surface, Park et al. supplies long-horizon coherent (and deliberately misalignable) agent personalities for red-teaming, CRUX supplies the evaluation rigor. Reuses all three papers already reviewed instead of a fourth, disconnected direction.
Feasibility bottom line: a full Cluster-1-grade testbed is still the heavy lift flagged earlier — that doesn't change. But the distilled-model fidelity study (direction a) is a legitimately light, fundable, standalone pilot on Park et al.'s existing codebase, without new infrastructure — and it's evidence of hands-on empirical capability, directly addressing the credibility gap flagged in What It Would Take From Us.
