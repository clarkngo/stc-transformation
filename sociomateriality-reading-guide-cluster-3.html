The cluster in 60 seconds

These papers share one worry: when AI takes over the explicit, visible parts of work, the practice that used to build human judgment can quietly disappear. None of them say this is inevitable. They name the conditions: whether people must still verify, justify, and take responsibility for what the AI produces.

💻 From your field

Junior engineers used to learn the codebase by writing boilerplate, fixing small bugs, and reading stack traces. If AI does all of that, how do they become the senior engineers who can tell when AI-written code is subtly wrong?

🍎 From your field

Students used to build judgment by drafting, getting stuck, and revising. If AI drafts for them, the product may look better while the skill underneath doesn't grow, unless the course makes them check, defend, and refine.

📈 From your field

New analysts used to learn the business by building the first draft of every model and memo. If AI does that, the organization may get faster output now and weaker judgment later.

Paper 1 of 5

Generative AI and Moral Deskilling in Vocational Education: A Dialectical Agency Perspective

Mai, A. Y., Syamsuddin, M. M., & Sairah Z, A. R. (2026). Journal of Vocational, Informatics and Computer Education, 4(3), 149–169. doi:10.66053/voice.v4i3.1221 (open access)

Core: scaffold vs. substituteConceptual, literature-basedAlso in Cluster 5

In one sentence

Whether generative AI builds or erodes learners' judgment depends on the interaction between how AI use is structured and how the learner engages with it.

The argument

The authors build a Dialectical Agency framework from philosophy of technology (Ellul, Ihde) and virtue ethics (Vallor). It has two poles: S1, the structural setup of AI use (for example, efficiency pressure), and S2, the learner's mode of engagement. AI works as a scaffold for judgment when verification and reflection are institutionally supported, and as a substitute when outputs are accepted without evaluation.

They derive four propositions and are candid that the two mixed cases, where structure and engagement point different ways, remain open. Their suggested test is a longitudinal intervention study within one vocational cohort.

Key terms

Moral deskilling
Losing practical and ethical judgment because the practice that builds it is displaced.
Phronesis
Practical wisdom: knowing what to do in a specific situation.
Scaffold vs. substitute
AI that supports building a skill vs. AI that replaces the practice.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

Code review is the scaffold. If the team requires every AI-written change to be explained and tested by the person submitting it, AI speeds learning. If AI code gets merged on “looks fine,” juniors stop building the judgment to catch bugs. Same tool; the structure and the habits decide.

🍎 Teacher

This is the CEAU argument in another vocabulary. If the assignment requires students to verify, justify, and take responsibility for AI output, AI scaffolds judgment. If only the final product is graded, AI substitutes for the thinking.

📈 Business

In training programs (welding, nursing, auditing), AI checklists can either build practical wisdom or replace it. The lever is institutional: sign-off rules, supervised verification, and accountability for AI-assisted decisions.

Why it matters for our paper

Very close to our argument. The scaffold-vs-substitute distinction and the call for structures that require verification and justification map directly onto CEAU's Evaluate and Understand & Refine stages. Cite with Cluster 1's relational agency.

Show answer

S1 is the structural setup of AI use; S2 is the learner's mode of engagement. Their interaction shapes whether AI scaffolds or substitutes for judgment.

2. When does AI act as a scaffold, according to the paper?

Paper 2 of 5

The Human Agency Deficit in AI-Enabled Knowledge Creation

Farrukh, M. (2026). Knowledge and Process Management. doi:10.1002/kpm.70165

Core: names the risk preciselyConceptual + structured reviewSECI model

In one sentence

AI doesn't have tacit knowledge, but by automating the explicit tasks through which people used to develop it, AI can create a gap between the judgment an organization needs and the judgment it actually builds.

The argument

Starting from Polanyi and Nonaka's SECI spiral, the paper reviews 15 models of AI-enabled knowledge creation (from a Scopus search of 192 records). All of them still rely on humans for tacit knowing, but rarely ask how AI use changes the way people form it over time.

The paper names the Human Agency Deficit: the gap between the human agency an organization needs to evaluate, own, and extend its knowledge, and the agency its AI-supported practices actually develop. It's a conditional risk, not an inevitable outcome, and the paper sets out the conditions under which AI works as a learning scaffold, plus five testable propositions.

Key terms

Tacit knowing
Know-how carried in experience that's hard to put into words.
SECI spiral
Socialization, externalization, combination, internalization: how knowledge moves between tacit and explicit.
Human Agency Deficit
Needed human agency minus the agency AI-supported practice actually develops.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

It's a skills version of technical debt. Every task AI does that a junior used to learn from is a small loan against the team's future ability to debug, design, and review. The interest comes due during the incident nobody understands.

🍎 Teacher

Writing the first draft was never just about the draft; it was how students learned to think in the subject. If AI writes it, the lesson needs another way to make that tacit learning happen, or students graduate with the output but not the know-how.

📈 Business

A board can ask: which formative tasks have we automated, and where will tomorrow's experts learn their judgment now? The paper's indicators turn this into something HR and knowledge managers can track.

Why it matters for our paper

A precise, citable name for the risk our framework addresses. CEAU can be presented as a design that closes the Human Agency Deficit by keeping the formative work (evaluate, analyze, refine) with the learner.

Check yourself

1. Does the paper claim AI has tacit knowledge?

Show answer

No. It agrees AI doesn't. The concern is that AI automates explicit tasks through which people used to develop tacit knowing.

2. Define the Human Agency Deficit.

Show answer

The gap between the human agency an organization needs to evaluate, own, and extend its knowledge and the agency its AI-supported practices actually develop and use.

Paper 3 of 5

One Ring to Rule Them All: An Allegory of Artificial Intelligence, Corruptive Drift, and the Limits of Organizational Governance

Saeedi, M. (2026). Proceedings of the 47th International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS 2026), Lisbon. Completed research paper.

Supporting: a vivid constructConceptual, allegoricalGovernance

In one sentence

AI's biggest effects on organizations may be the slowest: sustained use gradually reshapes professional judgment and the standards themselves, so audits keep passing while everyone drifts together.

The argument

The paper uses Tolkien's One Ring, which amplifies power while changing its bearer, as a lens. It opens with evidence that experts lean on AI even when they know it's unreliable, such as consultants who did better with AI on tasks inside its range and worse on tasks just outside it. Its core construct is corruptive drift: the gradual reshaping of judgment through sustained AI engagement.

A five-stage process model: power amplification, uneven usability, illusion of control, corruptive drift, and dependency and lock-in. The governance problem: frameworks built to catch discrete violations can't detect a change that moves the standard itself. The proposed counter-capability is strategic restraint.

Key terms

Corruptive drift
Gradual, hard-to-see reshaping of judgment and standards through sustained AI use.
Strategic restraint
The organizational capability to deliberately limit or not use AI where it would erode judgment.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

It's like a linter whose rules slowly change to match whatever the code already does. Every check passes, but the bar has moved. Drift in what “good code” means can't be caught by tests written against the drifted standard.

🍎 Teacher

If teachers use AI to grade AI-assisted work against rubrics AI helped write, every assessment can look fine while the standard for “good work” quietly shifts. Strategic restraint means keeping some assessment deliberately AI-free to anchor the bar.

📈 Business

Compliance audits check rule violations, not whether the rules themselves have drifted. If both auditor and audited lean on the same AI, "clean audit" can mean “drifted together.” Restraint has to be designed in, and it gets more expensive the deeper AI is integrated.

Why it matters for our paper

Corruptive drift is a strong supporting idea for why assessment needs anchors in human judgment. Keep the allegory light in our paper; the construct is what's useful.

Check yourself

1. Why can't conventional governance catch corruptive drift?

Show answer

Because it is designed to catch discrete violations against a stable standard, and drift moves the standard itself.

2. What capability does the paper propose in response?

Show answer

Strategic restraint: deliberately limiting or not using AI where it would erode judgment.

Paper 4 of 5

The Efficiency Trap of Agentic AI in Human Resource Management

Yang, M. M. (2026). Human Resource Management. doi:10.1002/hrm.70106 (open access)

Supporting: loose couplingConceptualAgentic AI, HR

In one sentence

Handing HR execution to agentic AI removes human variation and slack, which makes organizations efficient now but brittle in a crisis and weakens how future managers learn.

The argument

Drawing on Weick's idea of loose coupling, the paper argues that delegating HR execution to agentic AI creates algorithmic tight coupling. Removing human “implementation variance” gives a near-perfect fit in the short term, but strips out strategic slack, leading to structural calcification. Line managers are a buffer: their sensemaking helps organizations absorb shocks.

It also warns of a developmental cost: managers who never served “the apprenticeship of the routine” may lack the understanding to supervise the systems they're responsible for. The paper proposes a governance framework that balances machine consistency with human stewardship.

Key terms

Loose coupling
Parts of an organization connected flexibly, so a shock in one doesn't break everything.
Algorithmic tight coupling
When AI execution links processes so rigidly that there's no slack for judgment.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

It's microservices vs. a tightly coupled monolith. Removing every buffer and retry makes the happy path fast, but one unexpected input cascades everywhere. Human managers are the circuit breakers.

🍎 Teacher

Fully automated pacing (an AI that decides every student's next task) removes the teacher's discretion, the slack that lets a class absorb a bad week. And new teachers who never plan a unit lose the practice that builds their judgment.

📈 Business

Zero-touch HR looks great on cost dashboards. The hidden costs: no slack for a crisis, and a pipeline of managers who never learned the routine they now supervise. Keep humans in the loop where judgment, not consistency, is the point.

Why it matters for our paper

Adds an organizational argument for keeping humans in the loop: slack and apprenticeship. Useful for the EMBA and corporate-partnership angle more than for CEAU directly.

Check yourself

1. What does algorithmic tight coupling trade away?

Show answer

Strategic slack and the human sensemaking buffer that help organizations absorb shocks.

2. What developmental risk does the paper name?

Show answer

Managers who never did the routine work may lack the understanding to supervise the AI systems running it.

Paper 5 of 5

Used More, Trusted Less: AI Coding Assistants and Developer Trust in Stack Overflow Surveys from 2023 to 2026

Makara, S., & Chhim, N. (2026). Journal of Sociotechnical Inquiry, 1(2), 1–11.

Supporting: hard numbersSecondary survey analysis234,715 respondents

In one sentence

Professional developers' use of AI tools rose to 85% while their trust in AI output fell, because they use AI as a draft to check, not an answer to rely on.

The argument

Using the public Stack Overflow Developer Survey for 2023–2026 (234,715 respondents in total), the paper finds use among professional developers rose from 44.2% (2023) to 85.2% (2026). Among users, trust in AI output accuracy fell from 46.3% to 38.4%, and distrust rose from 25.1% to 38.0%. Delegation stayed narrow: in 2025, 58.3% of users wrote code with AI but only 6.6% let AI do most of it. In 2026, 98.2% reported checking AI output before using it.

The authors call this supervised use: adoption counts don't mean reliance. AI moves scarce effort from producing code to judging code. More experienced developers trusted AI less.

Key terms

Use–trust gap
How widely people use a tool vs. how far they trust its output.
Supervised use
Treating AI output as a draft to verify, not an answer to rely on.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

This is your own profession's data. "Almost right" AI code is the norm, so the job shifted from writing to reviewing. If your team measures AI success by suggestion acceptance rate, it's measuring the wrong thing.

🍎 Teacher

The professionals students are training to become don't trust AI blindly; they verify everything. A course that teaches students to accept AI output is teaching the opposite of professional practice. Teach verification as the skill.

📈 Business

Adoption dashboards ("85% of staff use AI") say nothing about value or risk. Ask instead: how much do we delegate, how much do we verify, and does verification eat the time AI was supposed to save?

Why it matters for our paper

Good empirical support for the claim that expert AI use is evaluation-heavy, which is CEAU's 20/80 split in professional form. Note the venue is a new journal; verify standing before citing.

Check yourself

1. What happened to developer trust in AI output as use rose?

Show answer

It fell: trust dropped from 46.3% to 38.4% and distrust rose from 25.1% to 38.0% among users.

2. What does “supervised use” mean here?

Show answer

Developers use AI widely but delegate narrowly, treating output as a draft to check; 98.2% reported checking before use in 2026.
