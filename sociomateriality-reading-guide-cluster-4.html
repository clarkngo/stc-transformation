The cluster in 60 seconds

Two of these papers are squarely about learning: one says literacy was always produced by people plus texts plus tools, and AI just makes that obvious; the other asks which AI skills count as a real competence. The other three look at workplaces (hybrid offices, platform rules, human–machine work environments). They're here because the same question applies to classrooms: who or what really controls the work, and how should the environment be designed?

💻 From your field

The classroom is a system with a configuration: the platform, the rules, the tools, the room. These papers ask which parts of that configuration you can design, and which ones quietly decide outcomes no matter what the syllabus says.

🍎 From your field

Your course is an environment, not just content. The LMS rules, the AI tools allowed, the room or the home desk all shape what students can do. These papers give you vocabulary for designing that environment on purpose.

📈 From your field

Training programs, hybrid offices, and digital platforms all decide more than managers think. These papers show where control really sits once systems and AI are involved, and how to design environments where people and machines work well together.

Paper 1 of 5

Foregrounded by AI: What a Sociomaterial Account of Literacy Implies for Postdigital Design Orientations

Schlauch, M. (2026). Postdigital Science and Education. doi:10.1007/s42438-026-00716-7 (open access)

Core: the education anchorConceptualAlso in Cluster 5

In one sentence

LLMs don't create a new literacy problem; they reveal that literacy was always produced by people, texts, technologies, and infrastructures together, not inside individual heads.

The argument

Instead of asking what new “AI literacy” students need, the paper asks what LLMs reveal about literacy itself. Drawing on Actor-Network Theory and sociomaterial education research, it argues literacy has always been emergent practice within assemblages. Competence frameworks that center the individual can't see the distributed, infrastructural, and non-human parts that AI now makes visible.

It derives three design orientations: make assemblages visible; sustain fluid literacy spaces; and preserve conditions for critical reflexivity. It also warns that current AI assemblages may widen educational inequality rather than benefit all learners equally.

Key terms

Assemblage
The network of people, texts, tools, and infrastructure through which literacy happens.
Design orientation
A guiding principle for designing learning environments, not a fixed rule.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

"Can this developer code?" was always partly "can this developer code with their IDE, docs, Stack Overflow, and teammates?" AI just makes the dependency impossible to ignore. Making the assemblage visible is like documenting your toolchain instead of pretending skill lives only in the person.

🍎 Teacher

Stop asking only “what AI skills should students have?” Start asking “what conditions should my course create?” The three orientations translate into practice: have students show which tools contributed what; leave room to work in different ways; build in moments to step back and critique the AI's role.

📈 Business

Corporate “AI upskilling” that only trains individuals misses the point. Performance comes from the whole setup: tools, data access, templates, and norms. Design the environment, and watch for who gets left behind by it.

Why it matters for our paper

Probably the single best education-side citation for Paper 1. It moves the unit of analysis from the individual learner to the assemblage, exactly our argument, and its “make assemblages visible” orientation supports CEAU's disclosure and reflection steps.

Check yourself

1. What does the paper say LLMs reveal about literacy?

Show answer

That it was always sociomaterial, constituted in more-than-human assemblages, not in individual cognition.

2. Name the three design orientations.

Show answer

Paper 2 of 5

AI Agent Reliance and Creator-Perceived Content Innovation in Digital Entrepreneurship

Wang, S., & Lu, M. (2027). The International Journal of Management Education, 25, 101576. doi:10.1016/j.ijme.2026.101576

Supporting: what counts as a competenceSurvey (415) + 35 interviewsManagement education

In one sentence

Among student entrepreneurs, relying on AI agents went with more (self-reported) content innovation, and only one skill, knowing how to steer AI's persuasive output, qualified as a candidate competence.

The argument

A three-wave survey of 415 Chinese university student entrepreneurs who make short-video content, plus 35 interviews. Reliance on AI agents was associated with creator-perceived innovation through two paths: cognitive offloading (freeing mental capacity) and producer-activated persuasion knowledge (knowing how to steer AI output toward novel, on-brand directions). Seeing the AI as human-like weakened both links.

The paper is careful: everything is self-reported and no teaching intervention was tested. Using five explicit criteria, only persuasion knowledge qualifies as a candidate competence, and the curriculum ideas are hypotheses.

Key terms

Cognitive offloading
Handing mental work to a tool to free attention for other things.
Candidate competence
A skill that meets stated criteria for being taught and assessed, but hasn't been proven through scored performance.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

The useful skill wasn't "using AI"; it was steering it: knowing how prompts and context push output in a direction. Like knowing a library's defaults well enough to override them on purpose.

🍎 Teacher

A good model of rigor: before calling something an “AI competence,” check it against explicit criteria. Most AI skills students pick up in practice aren't yet teachable competencies; one, steering the AI, might be.

📈 Business

For marketing and content teams: value came from people who could steer AI toward brand-fit novelty, not from heavy use alone. Hire and train for steering, and be wary of staff treating the AI like a colleague rather than a tool.

Why it matters for our paper

Methodologically useful: its explicit criteria for classifying a competence could inform how we justify CEAU's skills. Its caution about self-report is a reminder for Paper 2's measures.

Check yourself

1. What were the two pathways linking AI reliance to perceived innovation?

Show answer

Cognitive offloading effectiveness and producer-activated persuasion knowledge.

2. Why do the authors call it only a “candidate” competence?

Show answer

Because it qualified by argument from item content, not by scored performance, and no teaching intervention was tested.

Paper 3 of 5

The Future Workplace: Developing the Human–Machine Symbioscape

Sajtos, L., Le, K. B. Q., Rachakonda, N., & Kunz, W. H. (2026). Journal of Service Management. doi:10.1108/JOSM-11-2025-0643 (open access)

Supporting: designing the environmentConceptualService settings

In one sentence

The work environment itself (physical and digital, social, and institutional) shapes how well humans and AI-enabled machines learn to work together.

The argument

The paper extends the “servicescape” idea (how a store's environment shapes service encounters) to hybrid human–machine work. The symbioscape has three tiers: physical–digital, socio-relational, and institutional–ecosystem conditions. Across them, six cue categories shape what humans and AI can notice, how they coordinate, and how routines stick.

Using Weick's sensemaking (enactment, selection, retention), it links these tiers to how fast, how well, and how durably human–AI collaboration adapts. It's purely conceptual.

Key terms

Symbioscape
The designed environment in which humans and AI-enabled machines collaborate.
Adaptation speed, effectiveness, persistence
How fast, how well, and how lastingly human–AI collaboration adjusts.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

It's developer experience for human–AI teams. Physical–digital: is the agent's activity visible in the tools people already watch? Socio-relational: who reviews what, in what order? Institutional: what training and policies exist? All three decide whether the collaboration works.

🍎 Teacher

Your classroom (or LMS) is a symbioscape. Is the AI's role visible? How are student–AI–teacher handoffs sequenced? What policies and training back it up? Designing these three tiers matters as much as choosing the tool.

📈 Business

A diagnostic for frontline AI rollouts: check visibility and interface design, workflow sequencing and interaction load, and governance and role design. Weakness in any tier slows adaptation.

Why it matters for our paper

Supporting. Its three tiers could help structure Paper 2's description of the learning environment, but it's conceptual and service-focused.

Check yourself

1. Name the symbioscape's three tiers.

Show answer

Physical–digital, socio-relational, and institutional–ecosystem.

2. What existing idea does the symbioscape extend?

Show answer

The servicescape: how the physical environment shapes customer–employee encounters.

Paper 4 of 5

Platform Authority vs. Managerial Authority: Who Really Controls Work in Digitised Organisations

Kuika Watat, J., Masiero, S., & Jonathan, G. M. (2026). ICIS 2026 Proceedings. AIS eLibrary

Supporting: platform controlQualitativeHealth information platforms

In one sentence

When validation rules and system thresholds decide whether an action can go through, managers keep formal responsibility while practical control shifts to the platform and the technical people who broker it.

The argument

A qualitative, practice-based study of reporting, validation, and coordination on health information platforms. Three mechanisms: displacement (managerial authority comes apart from the ability to make decisions stick), mediation (technical intermediaries broker between what managers want and what the system will accept), and reconstitution (a provisional settlement forms around output the organization can use).

The key distinction is formal authority vs. operative authority. The practical advice: clarify who can change validation rules, document intermediaries' work, and set escalation paths.

Key terms

Operative authority
The practical ability to make a decision actually take effect.
Technical intermediary
The person who translates between managerial intent and system requirements.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

You've seen this: the VP decides, but the schema validation, the required fields, and the person who knows how to get a record past the checks decide what actually happens. Whoever owns the validation rules owns operative authority.

🍎 Teacher

The teacher decides the grade, but the LMS gradebook, rubric settings, and submission rules can block or reshape it. The instructional technologist who knows the workaround is a technical intermediary with real power over assessment.

📈 Business

Your org chart shows formal authority. Your systems' validation rules show operative authority. Map who can change the rules, and make the intermediaries' work visible; that's where control really sits.

Why it matters for our paper

Peripheral for CEAU, but a useful reminder that platforms (LMS, AI tools) carry authority over assessment. Worth a sentence in the sociomaterial framing.

Check yourself

1. Name the three mechanisms.

Show answer

Displacement, mediation, and reconstitution.

2. What's the difference between formal and operative authority?

Show answer

Formal authority is responsibility attached to a role; operative authority is the practical capacity to make a decision take effect.

Paper 5 of 5

The New Meaning of Office: How Hybrid Work Changes Formal Organizational Structure from a Place Perspective

Fuchs, C., & Reichel, A. (2026). Review of Managerial Science. doi:10.1007/s11846-026-01074-1 (open access)

Peripheral: place and structure49 interviews, two timesHybrid work

In one sentence

Hybrid work made the workplace feel virtual, which loosened how structure was tied to physical space and opened a window for reorganizing, while the office came back as a place for social contact.

The argument

From 49 interviews in two international organizations at two points in time, the authors found a trend toward grouping HR roles by function and centralizing them, sometimes followed by physical relocation. Their explanation uses a place lens: as people “re-made” place in virtual and physical spaces, they came to see the workplace as virtual, so the physical expression of formal structure dissolved, creating room for leaders to restructure.

But the virtual workplace didn't meet people's need for connection, so the office was reconstituted as a place for social contact, and relocation helped new teams form.

Key terms

Place-making
How people turn a space into a meaningful place through what they do there.
Formal structure
The prescribed grouping of positions and reporting lines.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

Once the team lives in Slack and GitHub, the floor plan stops defining the team, so reorgs get easier. But people still want an “office” for the social glue, so in-person days get redesigned around team bonding rather than desk work.

🍎 Teacher

Online and hybrid courses change what the “classroom” means. Once the course lives in the LMS, the room's role shifts toward connection: discussion, community, and working together, rather than content delivery.

📈 Business

Hybrid work gives leaders a window to restructure, because physical layout no longer locks in the org chart. Plan for the office as a place for connection, and use moves to cement new team structures.

Why it matters for our paper

Peripheral to the CEAU paper. Possibly useful only if we discuss online or hybrid delivery as part of the learning environment.

Check yourself

1. What structural trend did the study find?

Show answer

Functional grouping and centralization of previously decentralized HR positions, sometimes followed by relocation.

2. What new role did the physical office take on?

Show answer

A place for social contact, compensating for what the virtual workplace lacked.
