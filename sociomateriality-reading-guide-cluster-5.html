The cluster in 60 seconds

Despite the cluster's name, most of these papers are about classrooms. Together they mark the edges of when AI and other technology help learning: when teachers keep judgment and control, when use is structured rather than spontaneous, and when we're honest about what self-reports can and can't show.

💻 From your field

Boundary conditions are the preconditions in a function's contract: the inputs under which it behaves as promised. These papers try to write the contract for AI in classrooms, including where it breaks.

🍎 From your field

These are the “it depends” papers. AI helps critical reading when the task structures how it's used. Teachers stay effective when they keep judgment. And some costs, like AI's environmental footprint, stay invisible unless someone makes them visible.

📈 From your field

Every AI rollout has operating conditions. These papers find them for education: structure beats free use, people in the loop matter, and some costs never show up on the dashboard.

Paper 1 of 4

Ecological Invisibility in Classroom AI Use: Rethinking Pedagogical Efficiency, Sustainability and Teacher Agency

Uğraş, H., & Uğraş, M. (2026). Systems, 14(10), 1268. doi:10.3390/systems14101268 (open access)

Supporting: teacher agencyInterviews, 22 teachersTürkiye

In one sentence

Teachers who use AI know about its environmental cost, but their professional attention, focused on teaching well, keeps that cost out of everyday decisions.

The argument

Interviews with 22 public-school teachers in Türkiye who had at least a year of classroom AI use, analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis. Five themes: teachers actively mediate AI through pedagogical judgment, a restructured professional agency, and a renewed relational and epistemic role. The central contribution is the last two themes: AI's ecological cost is not unknown but invisible in practice, and when it surfaces, teachers manage rather than resolve it through pedagogical rationalization and stated intentions of restraint.

The authors frame ecological invisibility as a by-product of professional attention, not a knowledge gap. They're careful that “restraint” was a reported intention, not verified behavior change.

Key terms

Ecological invisibility
A known cost that doesn't register in everyday decisions because attention is organized elsewhere.
Pedagogical rationalization
Justifying AI use by its teaching benefits when its other costs come up.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

It's like cloud cost: everyone knows GPUs aren't free, but nobody sees the bill when they hit “regenerate.” Costs that aren't surfaced in the tool you use don't enter decisions. The fix is infrastructural visibility, like cost dashboards, not lectures.

🍎 Teacher

You probably know AI uses a lot of energy, but it never comes up when you're deciding whether to use it for a worksheet. That's the finding. The paper's suggestion is critical AI literacy that brings ethical, ecological, and teaching concerns into the same decision.

📈 Business

Sustainability targets and AI adoption targets live in different dashboards. Unless the environmental cost of AI use is visible where decisions are made, staff will keep rationalizing it away.

Why it matters for our paper

Supporting. Shows teacher agency as both the mechanism and the fix, a sociomaterial point. Relevant if our effective-AI construct includes resource costs (Jim's draft mentions "resource demands").

Check yourself

1. Is ecological invisibility a knowledge gap?

Show answer

Paper 2 of 4

How Does Generative AI Shape Doctoral Students' Critical Reading in Academic Contexts? A Mixed-Methods Investigation

Ma, L., Cao, M., & Sun, G. (2026). Journal of English for Academic Purposes, 84, 101792. doi:10.1016/j.jeap.2026.101792

Core: structured vs. spontaneous useMixed methods, 38 PhD studentsSociomateriality + activity theory

In one sentence

PhD students read more critically when their AI use was structured than when it was spontaneous, and the strong readers checked AI against the source and kept the final judgment for themselves.

The argument

38 STEM doctoral students in China completed three critical-reading tasks: two with spontaneous AI use, one with structured use. Cluster analysis found three usage profiles (high, selective, and low engagement). Structured use scored much higher (about 13.2 vs. 8.6 on the rubric).

Focus groups surfaced five interactional processes: pre-AI independent reading, AI output verification, iterative prompting, AI-assisted deep reading, and independent judgment.

Important caveat the authors state: the tasks ran in a fixed order and differed in text type and timing, so the structured task's advantage is confounded with practice. They call it exploratory, not causal.

Key terms

Structured AI use
AI use guided by set prompts and steps, versus whatever the student does on their own.
Independent judgment
The final step where the reader decides, rather than adopting the AI's view.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

The five processes are a code-review workflow for reading: read the diff yourself first, then run the AI reviewer, verify its claims against the code, iterate on prompts, and make the merge decision yourself. Spontaneous use skips the first and last steps.

🍎 Teacher

This is the strongest classroom evidence in the library for structuring AI use. The five processes are practically a lesson plan: read first, verify AI against the text, re-prompt, go deeper, decide yourself. Just remember the confound: the structured task came last.

📈 Business

For professional training: give staff a structured protocol for AI-assisted analysis (read first, verify, decide) rather than open access. But measure carefully: this study's design can't fully separate the protocol's effect from practice.

Why it matters for our paper

Very close to CEAU: pre-AI reading and independent judgment bracket AI use, which mirrors Create/Scaffold followed by Evaluate–Analyze–Understand & Refine. Also a cautionary model for Paper 2: counterbalance task order, or the effect is confounded.

Check yourself

1. Name the five interactional processes.

Show answer

Pre-AI independent reading, AI output verification, iterative prompting, AI-assisted deep reading, independent judgment.

2. Why can't the structured-use advantage be read as causal?

Show answer

The tasks were in a fixed order and differed in text type and timing, so the effect is confounded with practice and order.

Paper 3 of 4

Supporting Teacher Orchestration in Ubiquitous Learning Environments: A Study in Primary Education

Muñoz-Cristóbal, J. A., Jorrín-Abellán, I. M., Asensio-Pérez, J. I., Martínez-Monés, A., Prieto, L. P., & Dimitriadis, Y. (2015). IEEE Transactions on Learning Technologies, 8(1), 83–97. doi:10.1109/TLT.2014.2370634

Peripheral: pre-AI orchestrationInterpretive case studyPrimary school, 2015

In one sentence

A system called GLUEPS-AR helped a pre-service teacher coordinate a lesson spread across the classroom, the playground, and a park, with multiple devices, though keeping track of students outdoors stayed hard.

The argument

Before generative AI, this paper tackled orchestration: the teacher's work of coordinating activities, tools, and people across spaces. A pre-service teacher designed a five-session orienteering unit across classroom, playground, and park, using wikis, Web 2.0 tools, and augmented reality, all coordinated by GLUEPS-AR.

The system helped with design, runtime adaptation, and sharing orchestration work with students. Main weakness: teacher awareness during outdoor activities. Lessons: technology fails unpredictably across spaces, so changing the design on the fly, from wherever the teacher is, was critical.

Key terms

Orchestration
The teacher's real-time coordination of activities, tools, groups, and spaces.
Ubiquitous learning environment
Learning spread across physical and digital spaces with mobile devices.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

Orchestration is the teacher's version of deployment and ops across environments. GLUEPS-AR is like an orchestration layer that deploys one design across several platforms, with live config changes when something breaks in production (the park).

🍎 Teacher

Any multi-tool lesson needs orchestration. The useful lessons still hold with AI: expect tools to fail, be able to change the plan from your phone mid-activity, and share some orchestration with students. The weak spot, knowing what's happening where you can't see, is still unsolved.

📈 Business

Field training across sites faces the same issue: coordination load lands on the facilitator. Tools that let them adapt on the fly and delegate to participants matter more than features.

Why it matters for our paper

Peripheral. It predates generative AI and isn't sociomaterial in framing. Possibly relevant to Paper 2 if the low-resource delivery involves teachers coordinating across devices and places.

Check yourself

Show answer

2. What did the system struggle to support?

Show answer

Paper 4 of 4

Understanding the Relationship Between Digital Competence and Computational Thinking in Secondary Education

Fadillah, M. A., Husin, M., Akbar, M. F., Amini, R., Tanjung, S., & Usmeldi, U. (2027). Thinking Skills and Creativity, 63, 102420. doi:10.1016/j.tsc.2026.102420

Peripheral: self-report correlationsSurvey, 316 studentsIndonesia

In one sentence

Among 316 Indonesian high-school students, finding information, creating and adapting digital resources, and evaluating them were the digital skills most associated with computational thinking.

The argument

Using correlation analysis and machine-learning learning analytics on survey data, the study found three digital-competence dimensions most often top-ranked in association with computational thinking: Search, Find, Access; Develop, Apply, Modify; and Evaluate. Results varied for some sub-dimensions such as generalization.

The authors are explicit: both measures were self-reported and the analysis didn't control for covariates, so the findings reflect perceived competence and associations, not demonstrated skill or causation.

Key terms

Digital competence
Technical, critical, ethical, and social abilities for using digital technology.
Computational thinking
Problem-solving skills like decomposition, abstraction, and generalization.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

The three skills linked to computational thinking (find it, build or modify it, evaluate it) are the core developer loop: search docs, adapt code, test it. But this is self-reported survey data, so treat it as a hint, not a benchmark.

🍎 Teacher

A reasonable direction for curriculum (teach searching, making, and evaluating together) but weak evidence: students rated themselves. Use it to motivate, not to prove.

📈 Business

For digital-skills programs: the skills cluster together, so train them as a set. But self-assessed skill surveys don't tell you what people can actually do.

Why it matters for our paper

Peripheral. Mainly useful as a cautionary example for Paper 2: self-report measures can't stand in for demonstrated performance, which is why we plan blind-scored authentic tasks.

Check yourself

1. Which three digital-competence dimensions stood out?

Show answer

Search, Find, Access; Develop, Apply, Modify; and Evaluate.

2. What's the main limitation the authors stress?
