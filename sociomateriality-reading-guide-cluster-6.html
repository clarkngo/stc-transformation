The cluster in 60 seconds

A “technological frame” is the set of assumptions and expectations a group holds about a technology: what it is, why it's being introduced, and how it will be used. When different groups hold different frames (say, the people who chose a tool and the people who have to use it), trouble follows. These papers apply that idea from 1994 to today's AI.

💻 From your field

Frames are like mental models of a system. If the platform team thinks the new tool is “infrastructure for everyone” and the product teams think it's “another mandated chore,” adoption fails, no matter how good the tool is.

🍎 From your field

Students, teachers, and administrators each hold a frame of AI: a cheating tool, a tutor, a cost-saver. When the frames don't match, policies misfire. Surfacing them is the first step.

📈 From your field

Headquarters and the front line usually frame digital change differently: "a journey" vs. "a destination," augmentation vs. replacement. Diagnose the mismatch before the rollout.

Paper 1 of 4

Technological Frames: Making Sense of Information Technology in Organizations

Orlikowski, W. J., & Gash, D. C. (1994). ACM Transactions on Information Systems, 12(2), 174–207. doi:10.1145/196734.196745

Core: the founding paperCase study, Lotus Notes1994

In one sentence

Groups make sense of a technology through frames (assumptions, expectations, knowledge), and when technologists' and users' frames don't match, the technology gets used in unintended ways.

The argument

The paper introduces technological frames: the subset of people's understanding that concerns technology. It identifies three domains: nature of technology (what it is and can do), technology strategy (why it was brought in), and technology-in-use (how it will be used day to day).

In a case at a consulting firm called Alpha (the same Lotus Notes setting as Orlikowski 2000), technologists saw Notes as a platform for collaboration; users saw an email-like individual tool with worrying risks to confidentiality and liability. That incongruence helps explain why early use fell short of expectations.

Key terms

Technological frame
A group's shared assumptions, expectations, and knowledge about a technology.
Frame incongruence
When different groups' frames of the same technology don't line up.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

Classic platform-team problem: you built a collaboration platform; users see a glorified inbox with privacy risks. The three domains are a useful pre-launch checklist: what users think it is, why they think it was bought, and how they think they'll use it.

🍎 Teacher

Before introducing AI in a course, ask students what they think it is, why they think it's allowed, and how they'll use it. If their frame is “an answer machine the school permits,” your assignment design is fighting their frame.

📈 Business

Many failed IT rollouts are frame failures, not technology failures. Leaders frame the tool strategically; users frame it in terms of daily risk and effort. Surface both before go-live.

Why it matters for our paper

Core background for the sensemaking side of the framework, and it pairs with Orlikowski 2000 (same case). Learners' frames of AI could be a measured variable in Paper 2.

Paper 2 of 4

The Convergence Paradox: A Constructivist Account of Perceived Template Convergence and Originative Agency under Generative AI

Cao, Q., Zhou, Z., Wang, L., Liu, Y., & Li, Y. (2026). Frontiers in Psychology, 17, 1924628. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1924628 (open access)

Core: authorship and originalityConceptualAlso in Cluster 7

In one sentence

When people feel that AI is pushing everyone's ideas toward the same template, their confidence in producing competent work can rise while their sense of authorship and drive to create something original falls.

The argument

Worries about AI “homogenization” are usually stated about outputs or populations. This paper offers an individual-level construct: perceived template convergence (PTC), a person's sense that the ideas available through AI are collapsing toward a shared template.

Using personal construct theory, social cognitive theory, and research on meaningful work, it argues PTC is two-sided: the same appraisal that raises confidence can weaken authorship and meaning, steering effort toward imitation rather than origination. That's the convergence paradox, set out as a propositional model with two mediators and two boundary conditions, plus a measurement agenda.

Key terms

Perceived template convergence
A person's sense that AI-available ideas are converging on a common template.
Originative agency
The drive and capacity to create something genuinely new.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

If every team's AI-generated architecture doc looks the same, engineers may feel more confident shipping it and less like they designed anything. The paper's levers: tool literacy, rich prior knowledge, and preserving the experience of authorship.

🍎 Teacher

Students using AI may produce competent essays that feel like everyone else's, and stop trying to say something of their own. CEAU's “Create first” step is a direct countermeasure: originate before you converge.

📈 Business

AI can raise the floor of content quality while flattening differentiation. If staff feel their work is template output, meaning and ownership drop. Design roles that keep origination with people.

Why it matters for our paper

Directly supports CEAU's Create/Scaffold step: protecting authorship and origination. A good citation for why “create first” matters.

Paper 3 of 4

Rethinking Customer Service Digitalization: Incongruent Frames and Alignment Mechanisms in Retailing

Brock, J. K.-U., van Veen, M., Kramer, V., & Krafft, M. (2026). Journal of Service Research. doi:10.1177/10946705261498173

Supporting: frames today52 expert interviewsRetail

In one sentence

Retail headquarters see digital customer service as augmentation and an ongoing journey, while frontline staff see replacement and a fixed destination; four mechanisms help bridge the gap.

The argument

Fifty-two interviews with headquarters managers, store managers, and frontline employees, guided by technology framing theory. HQ frames digitalization as digitalization, human augmentation, and a continuous journey; frontline employees frame it as digitization, human substitution, and a fixed destination. Store managers sit between, as enablers or barriers.

Four collective congruence framing mechanisms buffer the damage: Ba (shared space for knowledge creation), psychological safety, redundancies, and nudging. Store managers acting as translators make them work better.

Key terms

Frame congruence
How closely different groups' frames of a technology align.
Boundary spanner
Someone who translates between groups, here store managers between HQ and the floor.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

Leadership says "AI will augment engineers and it's a journey." Engineers hear "we're being replaced and this is the end state." The fixes translate well: shared spaces to learn together, safety to raise concerns, keeping old paths (redundancy) during transition, and gentle nudges.

🍎 Teacher

Administrators may frame AI as augmenting teachers; teachers may hear replacement. Department chairs are the boundary spanners. Psychological safety and keeping familiar methods available during the change help close the gap.

📈 Business

A practical playbook: diagnose frame incongruence before rollout, use HQ store days and temporary redundancies, and develop middle managers as translators between strategy and frontline practice.

Why it matters for our paper

Supporting. Shows frame incongruence is alive in AI-era rollouts, and the four mechanisms could inform how a partner community's frames are surfaced in Paper 2's co-design phase.

Paper 4 of 4

Public Managers' Attitudes Towards Biometric Systems: An Exploratory Cluster Analysis

Chassot, C., Guenduez, A. A., & Mettler, T. (2026). Proceedings of the 12th International Conference on eDemocracy & eGovernment (ICEDEG). IEEE. doi:10.1109/ICEDEG70169.2026.11695447

Peripheral: frames in governmentSurvey, 297 managersSwitzerland

In one sentence

Swiss public managers fall into three attitude profiles toward biometric systems (enthusiasts, skeptics, and pragmatists), and only security is a rationale all of them accept.

The argument

Using technological frames theory and cluster analysis of survey data from 297 Swiss public managers, the study identifies Biometric Enthusiasts, Techno-Skeptics, and Pragmatists. Security is the only rationale for which established applications are supported across all profiles; operational performance, personalization, and health draw mixed responses, with personalization least supported.

Divergent frames across administrative levels can create friction and enable function creep, so the authors call for adaptive, accountable governance with external scrutiny.

Key terms

Justificatory rationale
The official reason given for adopting a technology (security, efficiency, and so on).
Function creep
A technology gradually being used for purposes beyond its original justification.

From your field

💻 Software engineer

Your team's attitude to a new auth system probably splits the same way: enthusiasts, skeptics, pragmatists. Security arguments win everyone; "personalization" arguments don't. And watch for function creep once the data exists.

🍎 Teacher

Faculty attitudes to AI proctoring or analytics likely cluster similarly. Integrity arguments may get broad support; personalization arguments less so. Watch for tools adopted for one purpose being used for others.

📈 Business

Segment your managers' frames before rolling out sensitive tech. The one rationale that bridges camps (often security or compliance) can carry adoption, but governance must stop function creep.

Why it matters for our paper

Peripheral: a sensitive-technology application of frames theory. Its function-creep point is relevant to data governance in Paper 2, especially with community and Indigenous data sovereignty.
